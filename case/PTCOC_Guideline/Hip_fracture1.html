Physiotherapy Guidelines for Geriatric Hip Fracture Rehabilitation

A working summary of the acute-to-community rehabilitation pathway for patients aged 65+ with a hip fracture, built to orient junior staff to assessment priorities, red flags, and phase-by-phase intervention before their first hip fracture caseload.

Source: Hospital Authority Head Office, Document No. HAHO-COC-GL-PHY-013-v01, Version 1.0, effective 25/11/2025

Overview & Journey

Why this matters and who it applies to, before you look at any single phase in isolation.

Scope

Applies to: patients with a hip fracture (femoral neck, inter-trochanteric, or sub-trochanteric fracture of the femur) aged 65 or above.

Excluded from this guideline: patients receiving total hip arthroplasty, patients with a pathological fracture, and patients with multiple fractures — these need a different orthopaedic and rehabilitation approach.

Most reliable predictors of outcome: pre-morbid mental state, mobility, and function — use these as your early screening tools for rehab needs and potential (SIGN Guidelines 2009).

Why it's a priority

~7,000patients admitted to HA per year for hip fracture
70%transfer rate from acute to extended care
35.3 daysaverage length of stay for those transferred to extended care
~80%of patients are aged ≥75 with multiple comorbidities
×4increase in mortality risk within 3 months of fracture
20–30%die within the 1st year after hip fracture
22.4% / 16.4%HK 1-year age-adjusted mortality, men / women (2009)
4.2%30-day mortality upon discharge

The estimated total annual number of hip fractures is projected to double from 2018–2050 as the population ages. A key HA performance indicator (KPI) is the percentage of patients having surgery ≤2 days after admission through A&E.

Level B evidence

Coordinated multidisciplinary rehabilitation

Patients with hip fracture should be offered a coordinated multidisciplinary rehabilitation programme with the specific aim of regaining sufficient function to return to their pre-fracture living arrangements (Mak et al 2010).

Fracture Types & Surgery

The precautions you teach only make sense if you know which structures the surgery went through — this is the "why" behind Section 3.

Intracapsular vs. extracapsular

Intracapsular (femoral neck): proximal to the attachment of the hip joint capsule to the femur at the inter-trochanteric line.

Extracapsular (inter-trochanteric): traverses the femur within the area bounded by the inter-trochanteric line proximally, up to 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter.

Undisplaced intracapsular

Fixation: internal fixation with screws, to stabilise the fracture and minimise avascular necrosis (AVN), non-union, or delayed union.

Displaced intracapsular

Fixation: unipolar or bipolar hemi-arthroplasty (surgeon's choice/patient criteria). Approach — southern, lateral, or Hardinge — affects different muscle groups, which has direct implications for your PT training plan.

Extracapsular (inter-trochanteric)

Fixation: sliding hip screw (SHS) — also called compression hip screw (Dynamic, Richards, or Ambi hip screw). A screw runs up the femoral neck to the head and attaches to a side plate; it's "dynamic" because it can slide/collapse at the fracture site. Allows earlier mobilisation and avoids recumbence.

Extracapsular (sub-trochanteric)

Fixation: intramedullary nail with a side screw into the femoral head (e.g. Gamma nail, Intramedullary Hip Screw, Kuntscher Y nail). Less common in the elderly.

Post-hemiarthroplasty

Hip precautions after hemiarthroplasty surgery

In a posterior or postero-lateral approach, the prosthetic hip can dislocate when the hip is held in a combination of excessive flexion (>90°), adduction, and internal rotation. Teach the patient and care-giver every one of these:

  • Avoid sitting in low chairs
  • Sit with the hips higher than the knees — elevate seat height or put the affected leg forward
  • Do not put your foot on a stool when sitting
  • Do not lean forward while sitting
  • Transfer to the side of the unaffected leg if necessary
  • Don't pivot when standing
  • Do not cross your legs when sitting, lying, or standing
  • Put a pillow between your legs when lying on your unaffected side
  • Do not bend down to pick things up from the ground — use a "grabber"; modify squatting with the affected leg in half-kneeling if physically able

A hip abduction pillow or Russell's sling may be used for a few days post-op, though there is no concrete evidence this reduces dislocation rate.

Preventing Complications

Three complication areas the physiotherapist must act on proactively — these are the red flags of this guideline.

1. Chest infection

Chest physiotherapy should start pre-operatively and continue throughout the acute phase.

Check:

  • Breathing status (shortness of breath)
  • Air entry, added sounds (wheeze/crackle)
  • Cough effort and cough sound
  • CXR, temperature, SpO₂ (screen for pneumonia)

Intervene:

  • Active breathing and coughing exercise
  • Postural drainage
  • Percussion, vibration, suction if indicated

Continue chest PT throughout rehab for patients with co-morbid cardiac/pulmonary problems (e.g. COPD).

Emergency

2. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Check for these signs after hip surgery:
  • Ipsilateral calf swelling
  • Increased hardness and temperature of calf muscle
  • Severe pain elicited on ankle dorsiflexion
DVT is a medical emergency — a dislodged thrombus can embolise to the lungs or brain. Timely medical management is required.

Also check past history of DVT and INR (normal range for a healthy person: 0.9–1.3; for people on warfarin therapy: 2.0–3.0).

Prevention: early mobilization is key — ankle/toes exercise, lower limb mobilization, and walking exercise started as early as possible.

Mechanical prophylaxis (NICE Guideline NG89, 2023): intermittent pneumatic compression (sequential compression device) can be considered at admission if pharmacological prophylaxis is contraindicated; anti-embolism stockings can be considered too, when not contraindicated — continue until the patient no longer has significantly reduced mobility.

Red flag

3. Prosthetic hip joint dislocation

Check for these signs:
  • Significant leg length discrepancy
  • Significant weakness on hip control
  • Internally rotated hip joint
  • Severe pain
  • Unable to bear weight over the hip joint

Mechanism: combined excessive flexion (>90°), adduction, and internal rotation in a posterior/postero-lateral approach. Educate patient and care-giver on the full precaution list — see the Fracture Types & Surgery tab.

Also stay alert to

Wound problems, pressure sores, and sciatic nerve palsy — maintain timely communication with medical, nursing, and other healthcare professionals about all of the above.

Acute Care Phase

Pre-operative/non-operated care, then post-operative care — assessment first, then intervention.

Pre-operative / non-operated case

Subjective

  • Present history of illness
  • Past medical history
  • Social history
  • Pre-morbid mental state, mobility, function
  • Level of home care support
  • Main complaint incl. pain over fracture site

Objective

  • Vital signs, consciousness, mental status
  • Chest conditions
  • Circulatory condition of lower limbs
  • Screening for DVT signs
  • AROM/strength of unaffected joints
  • Sensation of lower limbs; bed mobility

Intervention

  • Chest physiotherapy to prevent respiratory complications
  • Ankle and toe exercises to improve circulation and minimise DVT risk
  • Anti-embolism stocking / IPC prophylaxis until mobility is no longer significantly reduced
  • Uninvolved-limb mobilisation and strengthening to maintain ROM and minimise muscle atrophy
  • Bed mobility exercise to prevent pressure sores
  • Electro-physical agents for pain relief as indicated
  • Patient/care-giver education on proper transfer and care
NICE CG124, 2023

Post-operative phase

Note date/type of operation, radiological findings, and doctors' plan including weight-bearing status before you start.

Objective (in addition to pre-op items)

  • Screening of neurological deficits
  • Leg length discrepancy / mal-positioning
  • AROM and muscle strength
  • Functional mobility: bed mobility, transfer, sit/stand balance, ambulatory status incl. walking aids

Intervention

  • Chest PT, ankle/toe exercises, DVT prophylaxis continued
  • Active/active-assisted/passive mobilization of the operated hip
  • Strengthening of major operated lower-limb muscle groups
  • Electro-physical agents/manual therapy/exercise for pain and oedema
Early mobilization — including bedside transfer, standing, and walking — should start the day after surgery, unless medically or surgically contraindicated, and be offered at least once a day (NICE Guideline CG124, 2023).

Also: education to patient and care-giver in arthroplasty precautions, proper transfer/ambulation support, and limb maintenance exercise; prescription of suitable walking aids can be considered for indicated patients at this stage.

Extended Care Phase

The patient has moved from acute care but rehab intensity should not drop — this is where most functional gains and discharge prep happen.

Assessment

Same subjective and objective structure as the acute post-op phase (history, social history, pre-morbid mobility status, functional mobility: bed mobility/transfer/sitting-standing balance/ambulatory status), reassessed at this stage.

Intervention

  • Chest PT for patients with respiratory problems; continued DVT prevention (ankle/toe exercise, prophylaxis until mobility normalises)
  • Early mobilization exercise to minimise DVT and pressure sore risk
  • Active/assisted-active/passive mobilization to improve or maintain ROM of the operated limb
  • Strengthening exercise to improve/maintain muscle strength and endurance of the operated hip
  • Mobilization offered at least once a day, unless contraindicated (NICE Guideline CG124, 2023)
  • Upper-limb aerobic exercise to maintain/enhance cardiopulmonary fitness early in rehab
  • Balance training started early to enhance stability
  • Electro-physical agents/manual therapy/exercise to control pain and oedema
  • Care-giver education on proper transfer and ambulation skills for patients needing assistance at discharge
  • Stair and slope training as indicated
  • Home exercises and fall prevention programme given before discharge
  • Appropriate walking aids prescribed before discharge
  • Referral to community rehabilitation facilities if needed for reintegration
  • Telehealth via HA GO as an option for indicated patients

Community & Discharge

Community / ambulatory / supported discharge phase — the goal shifts from restoring basic function to safe re-integration.

Assessment

Subjective

  • Present history incl. previous fall history
  • Patient's self-perceived ability in self-care
  • Care-giver level of stress, if indicated
  • Level of re-engagement in social activities
  • Accommodation details

Objective

  • Physical: neurovascular status, deformity/LLD, joint mobility, strength/motor control
  • Functional: bed mobility, transfer, balance, indoor/outdoor ambulation incl. kerb/stairs/slope
  • Home environment safety; compliance with home exercise programme
  • Care-giver skills; condition of walking aids/wheelchair; community reintegration

Functional training

  • Strengthening / mobilization exercises
  • Transfer and walking training with suitable walking aids
  • Balance & gait training
  • Endurance and cardiovascular training (Mendelsohn et al 2008)
  • Advanced functional training: slope/stairs, escalator, uneven ground, road crossing

Home safety

  • Home assessment
  • Home modification and advice
  • Fall prevention programmes

Care-giver empowerment

  • Education for patients and care-givers
  • Handling techniques
  • Post-operative precautions
  • Community resources for sustaining mobility level

Telehealth via HA GO can be an option for exercise and care enhancement for indicated patients throughout this phase.

Outcome Measures Toolkit

Quick reference for the tools used across the journey — full scoring detail is in the guideline's appendices; this is what you need to interpret a score fast.

MFAC

Modified Functional Ambulation Classification — 7 stages, does not account for aids used
I Recliner → II Sitter → III Dependent Walker
IV Assisted Walker → V Supervised Walker
VI Indoor Walker → VII Outdoor Walker

EMS

Elderly Mobility Scale — 7 items, total /20
14–20: can discharge home, independent with basic ADL
10–13: needs high levels of care
<10: needs highest level of help with ADL

TUG

Timed Up and Go — result = 2nd trial time
<10s: freely independent
<20s: independent transfers, climbs stairs, goes out alone
>30s: dependent in most activities
>14s (community-dwelling, Shumway-Cook 2000): high fall risk

BBS

Berg Balance Scale — 14 items, total /56
Healthy 70-year-old: ~56 pts, declining ~0.7 pts/yr
No single cut-off predicts falls reliably
≤40 may indicate need for a mobility device

NPRS

Numeric Pain Rating Scale — 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible)
Rate during rest and during activity (bed mobility, transfers, weight bearing)

Handgrip strength

JAMAR dynamometer, 3 trials each hand, max reading recorded
Low strength cut-off: <28.0 kg (men), <18.0 kg (women) — AWGS 2019
Linked to higher mortality, prolonged time to ambulation, poorer 6-month functional independence

// 00 — Why this technique exists

Secretions, atelectasis, and a disconnected circuit problem

Intubated patients on mechanical ventilation are prone to atelectasis, secretion retention, and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) as mucociliary clearance breaks down. Hyperinflation — a slow deep breath in, fast release out — has been used to counter this since the 1960s.

Manual Hyperinflation

MHI — bag squeezing

A slow manual inspiration with a quick bag release generates an expiratory-over-inspiratory flow bias that moves secretions toward the proximal airway. Well studied and effective — but it requires disconnecting the patient from the ventilator circuit.

Circuit disconnection risks loss of PEEP and exposure to droplet / airborne pathogens.
Ventilator Hyperinflation

VHI — the ventilator does the work

VHI simulates MHI by altering ventilator settings rather than the circuit itself — no bag, no disconnection. A systematic review found VHI and MHI have similar effects on secretion clearance, compliance, and oxygenation.

This guideline covers when — and how — to reach for VHI instead.
Definition

"VHI is a chest physiotherapy technique in which larger tidal volume breaths are delivered using the ventilator itself."

Aims of lung hyperinflation

Mobilise secretion

Toward proximal airways by generating a flow bias where inspiratory flow < 90% of expiratory flow.

Re-inflate atelectasis

Partially collapsed alveoli reopen via increased pressure or volume, held with an inspiratory pause.

Improve oxygenation

A direct downstream effect of re-recruitment and improved compliance.

Improve V/Q matching

Better regional lung ventilation improves ventilation/perfusion matching.

Benefits of VHI over MHI — because the circuit stays closed

Maintains PEEP — prevents alveolar de-recruitment

Prevents cross-infection of droplet / airborne pathogens

Reduces risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)

Allows real-time, full monitoring of respiratory parameters

// 01 — When to reach for VHI

Indications

Consider VHI when a patient needs secretion mobilisation or re-inflation of atelectasis, and has one or more of the following:

01

High PEEP level ≥ 10 cmH₂O

02

Fraction of inspired oxygen FiO₂ ≥ 0.7

03

Pulmonary multi-drug resistance and/or airborne pathogens

04

Immunocompromised patient

05

Close monitoring of respiratory parameters during hyperinflation is highly desirable

06

Patient is PEEP-dependent

// 02 — Safety gate

Contraindications

VHI is not without risk. Screen every patient against these three tiers before proceeding — the tier determines what you're allowed to do next.

Do not proceed. If any one of these is present, VHI is contraindicated — full stop, regardless of how strong the indication is.
  • Documented cystic lung changes (bullae/blebs) — e.g. severe COPD with large emphysematous bullae, or cavitating lung pathology
  • Subcutaneous emphysema
  • Undrained pneumothorax
  • Bronchopulmonary fistula
  • Unexplained haemoptysis
  • PEEP ≥ 15 cmH₂O
  • Peak airway pressure ≥ 35 cmH₂O
  • Acute bronchospasm
  • Unstable haemodynamics — systolic BP ≤80 mmHg, or uncontrolled arrhythmia
  • ICP > 20 mmHg
Weigh risk vs. benefit. Proceed only with clinical judgement and team discussion.
  • Diagnosis of ARDS / fibrosis / other conditions with reduced lung compliance
  • Obstructing airway tumour
  • Acute pulmonary oedema
  • Large undrained pleural effusion
  • PEEP > 12 cmH₂O
  • Severe ARDS with lung-protective ventilation
Escalate first. These patient groups are high risk — get advice from the ICU physician before proceeding.
  • Patients receiving ECMO
  • Severe COPD
  • Recent cardiac or thoracic surgery
  • Drained pneumothorax
  • Fractured ribs / flail segment
  • Acute head injury
  • Dependent on PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O
  • Oxygen requirement ≥ 70%
// 03 — Clinical Implementation

The workflow, start to finish

Six steps from the guideline, grouped into before, during, and after the procedure.

Before
  1. Train & read. Staff are trained to the agreed standard and have read this guideline carefully.
  2. Communicate. Liaise with the medical team and nurse-in-charge to promote mutual understanding.
  3. Discuss indicated cases with the physiotherapist-in-charge.
  4. Discuss complicated cases with both the physiotherapist-in-charge and the physician-in-charge.
Gate & During
  1. Go through the checklists — Initial Assessment Checklist and Potential Complications & Remedial Actions — before commencing.
  2. Suction to remove secretions using a closed suction system.
Full checklists are covered in Wrap-Up.
After
  1. Resume original settings on the ventilator.
  2. Double-check with the bedside nurse that settings are correctly restored.
// 04 — Application of the Technique

Delivering the breath

Eight steps, applied in sequence, every time.

1
Determine need for VHI and check contraindications.
2
Note all baseline vital signs (BP, HR, SpO₂, EtCO₂) and ventilator settings.
3
Ensure upper pressure limit is set at 40 cmH₂O.
4
Position the patient accordingly.
5
Perform VHI according to ventilation mode.
6
Apply chest physiotherapy manoeuvres in synchrony with breath cycles.
7
Apply in sets from 6 breaths up to a few minutes; reassess the need for repeated cycles.
8
Return ventilator settings to baseline and check with the bedside nurse.

How to dial it in, by ventilation mode

MHI achieves its effect through slow hand inspiration, stepwise bag squeezing, an inspiratory hold, then a quick release. VHI reproduces each phase through ventilator settings — the adjustment depends on which mode the patient is in.

Expiratory flow bias(slow inspiration)
Consider switching to volume-targeted mode · decrease RR · increase I:E ratio · slow inspiratory flow by increasing duration to reach desired pressure
Hyperinflation
Stepwise increase of pressure control, keeping PIP within the pressure limit, until target TV is reached

Note: unlike MHI's sudden bag release, VHI never achieves a true "quick expiration" phase in any mode — the flow bias during inspiration is what does the work of secretion mobilisation instead.

// 05 — Eyes on the monitor, throughout

Monitoring

Both respiratory and haemodynamic parameters need continuous attention for the full duration of VHI application.

Respiratory condition
  • Airway pressure < 40 cmH₂O and plateau pressure < 35 cmH₂O — to avoid barotrauma
  • Tidal volume attained < 1000 mL
  • Minute volume — avoid hyper- or hypo-ventilation; match the patient's baseline
  • Peak expiratory and peak inspiratory flow rate
  • Lung compliance
  • Respiratory rate
  • Scalar of pressure, flow and volume — watch for abnormal abrupt changes
  • Breathing pattern — check for dyssynchrony with the ventilator
  • SpO₂
  • End-tidal carbon dioxide
Haemodynamics
  • Heart rate and blood pressure
  • Any new changes in cardiac rhythm
  • Intracranial pressure (if present) — kept within the range ordered by the neurosurgeon
// 06 — Stopping safely & charting it

Cessation, complications & documentation

When to stop

Planned stop

After an optimal number of sets have been applied and secretions have been cleared by suctioning.

Safety stop

If any contraindication or potential complication (below) develops during the procedure.

Potential complications & remedial action

Haemodynamic or respiratory instability

BP/pulse outside normal values, ICP beyond the ordered range, bronchospasm, marked change in respiratory rate, or desaturation.

Resume ventilator settings to baseline. Inform the nurse-in-charge and physician-in-charge for further management.
Patient agitation / non-cooperation

Patient becomes agitated even after explanation, and cannot cooperate with VHI application.

Cease the intervention and resume baseline ventilatory settings. Inform the nurse-in-charge for further management.

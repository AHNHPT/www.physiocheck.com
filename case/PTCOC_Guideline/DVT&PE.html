Introduction & scope

Two clots.
One leg. One lung.
Your call at the bedside.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) — deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) — is common, under-recognised in Asian populations, and squarely within a physiotherapist's decision-making every time you decide whether to mobilise a patient. This guide condenses the HA physiotherapy guideline into a working reference: who's at risk, how to examine, how to reason about mechanical and pharmacological prophylaxis, and — most importantly — when it is and isn't safe to get a patient up.

02 — Examine

Two bedside tests physiotherapists actually perform

Neither test is diagnostic alone — both feed into your overall clinical picture alongside the Wells score and the medical team's investigations.

Homans sign

SupportSupport the patient's thigh with one hand and the foot with the other.
PositionBend the leg slightly at the knee.
TestFirmly and abruptly dorsiflex the ankle.
InterpretDeep calf pain on dorsiflexion = a positive Homans sign.
Use with caution

Homans sign alone is considered unreliable. Treat it as one input alongside more accurate, specific procedures such as ultrasonography and venography.

Limb circumference comparison

Use a measuring tape 10cm below the tibial tuberosity, and always compare against the opposite leg.

Unaffected sidebaseline
Symptomatic side+3cm or more
Positive sign

A symptomatic-side circumference ≥3cm greater than the asymptomatic side indicates a positive sign of DVT.

03 — Prevent

Mechanical prophylaxis for patients at risk

The goal of mechanical prophylaxis is simple: keep venous blood moving. Four tools do this — movement itself, compression stockings, pneumatic compression, and, in select high-risk cases, an IVC filter.

Active, non-instrumental

Mobilisation & ambulation

Physical movement enhances circulation and prevents clot formation. There is strong evidence supporting a culture of mobility and physical activity for patients at risk of VTE — or already diagnosed with a lower-extremity DVT.

Percutaneous

Inferior vena cava (IVC) filter

Placed by an interventional radiologist (via right internal jugular or right femoral vein) for patients at high risk of PE who have contraindications to anticoagulants, or for whom medication hasn't been effective.

Mobilise once

Haemodynamically stable, and no bleeding at the puncture site.

Graduated compression elastic stockings (GCES)

Grade B — general & post-op abdominal surgery

Anti-embolism stockings conforming to the Sigel pressure profile reduce the rate of DVT by roughly 50%, and increase blood flow at the femoral vein by about 38%. They also aid oedema control by promoting venous and lymphatic return. In cancer patients, prescription of GCES is a good-practice point.

Indicated for

  • Leg symptoms, varicose veins, chronic venous deficiency
  • Thrombosis or embolism risk, non-venous oedema
  • Pregnancy, venous circulatory disorders
  • High clinical probability of developing DVT

Contraindicated in

  • Massive oedema, gangrenous limb, recent graft
  • Arteriosclerosis, cardiac failure
  • Severe PAD with ABPI < 0.8
  • Limb deformity / out-of-size limb, severe peripheral neuropathy
Fitting & monitoring checklist

Correct size and length · check skin colour, capillary refill and pressure points regularly · avoid creases or folds (tourniquet effect / pressure necrosis) · replace as limb circumference or elasticity changes · wean once fully mobile · issue the "Precautions for Elastic Stocking Application" fact sheet at discharge.

Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) / sequential compression devices (SCD)

Grade B–C

Mechanical inflatable devices that improve venous circulation in limbs affected by oedema or at risk of DVT. Supported by evidence for total hip replacement, hip fracture surgery, general surgery, and cardiac, thoracic and vascular surgery. Apply to the lower extremities until the patient is fully mobile.

04 — Decide

Managing a confirmed DVT: should you mobilise today?

Bed rest has no proven benefit in DVT and is associated with worse outcomes than early mobilisation. NICE also does not support GCES specifically to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome or VTE recurrence after a DVT (this doesn't rule out stockings for managing leg symptoms). The decision to mobilise instead hinges on anticoagulation status.

Evidence
  • Early mobilisation is associated with a lower incidence of new PE or DVT progression compared with bed rest.
  • There is no benefit to prescribing bed rest in patients with DVT.
  • Immediate mobilisation with compression in the acute stage reduces the incidence and severity of post-thrombotic syndrome.
  • Supervised exercise reduces calf circumference in patients with DVT.
Bottom line

With adequate monitoring and safety measures, mobilise patients with lower-extremity DVT as early as during the hospital stay — once therapeutic anticoagulant levels have been reached. Below is that decision logic as a live tool.

05 — Understand

The pharmacological landscape

Anticoagulation is the primary pharmacological treatment for DVT — different agents act at different points in the coagulation cascade to stop a clot forming or extending. Individuals typically continue anticoagulation for 3–6 months after a first VTE episode.

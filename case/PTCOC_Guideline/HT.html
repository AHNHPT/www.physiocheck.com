02 — Why Physiotherapy

The evidence base for exercise as antihypertensive therapy

Physical inactivity, high sodium intake, tobacco/alcohol use, and overweight/obesity are WHO-recognised modifiable risk factors — all directly addressable through physiotherapy-led exercise prescription and education.

5–7mmHg

Aerobic training

Consistently lowers resting blood pressure across meta-analyses of RCTs — an effect comparable to most single antihypertensive medications.

2–3mmHg

Dynamic resistance training

Reduces BP via increased nitric oxide synthesis and reduced sympathetic/adrenaline discharge. Moderate-to-vigorous intensity (>60% 1RM), ≥2×/week, ≥8 weeks shows the strongest effect.

≤24hours

Post-exercise hypotension

A single 30–50 minute bout of aerobic exercise, especially at vigorous intensity, can reduce ambulatory BP for up to 24 hours — more pronounced in patients with hypertension.

4–54mmHg SBP

Slow breathing exercise

Respiratory rate <10 breaths/min with prolonged, slow, deep expiration increases baroreflex sensitivity. Reported reductions: SBP 4–54.22 mmHg, DBP 3–17 mmHg.

1mmHg / kg

Weight reduction

Roughly 1 mmHg SBP reduction per kilogram of weight lost in overweight/obese patients. A ≥5% weight loss is recommended to meaningfully improve BP and cholesterol.

Tai Chi — the adherence-friendly alternative

Aerobic exercise is evidence-based, but adherence in hypertensive patients is reported at under one-third. Tai Chi is safe, low-impact, inexpensive, and equipment-free — multiple systematic reviews support its antihypertensive effect via reduced sympathetic excitability.

HIIT — promising, but progress gradually

High-Intensity Interval Training shows benefit for BP control in some studies, but progression for hypertensive patients should always be gradual — avoid large jumps in Frequency, Intensity, Time, or Type at once.

03 — Safety-Critical

Designing the physiotherapy exercise program

Before any prescription: confirm the patient fits the target group, screen out exclusion criteria, and understand exactly how abnormal blood pressure and medications change your plan.

Objectives

  • Improve health status and promote active lifestyle through self-management
  • Empower overweight/obese patients with weight-management knowledge
  • Prevent or reduce disease-related complications
  • Build patient knowledge of exercise therapy for hypertension

Target patients

  • Patients with hypertension under regular follow-up in HA GOPCs
  • Patients with overweight or obesity

Exclusion criteria

6.4.1 Abnormal Blood Pressure +
⛔Absolute stop: if resting SBP >200 mmHg and/or DBP >110 mmHg, no exercise should be performed that session.

Seek medical clearance first

Patients with Stage 2 hypertension (SBP ≥160 mmHg or DBP ≥100 mmHg) or complications such as left ventricular hypertrophy or diabetic retinopathy should seek medical advice before exercise. A medically supervised, symptom-limited exercise test is recommended before starting the program.

During training

Maintain SBP <220 mmHg and/or DBP ≤105 mmHg throughout the session. For patients with unstable blood pressure, check BP between different exercise modes.

Valsalva maneuver

Breath-holding against a closed glottis (e.g. straining) can trigger extreme BP spikes, dizziness, and fainting. Cue patients to exhale during exertion, especially in resistance training — never hold the breath.

6.4.2 Impact of Drug Effect +

Beta-blockers

Attenuate the heart-rate response to exercise, so target-HR formulas (e.g. 220-age) become unreliable. Use Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) instead to gauge and prescribe intensity.

Alpha-blockers, calcium channel blockers, vasodilators

May cause a sudden drop in blood pressure after exercise. Extend and closely monitor the cool-down — more than 10 minutes of light-to-moderate cardiorespiratory and muscular-endurance activity is advised.

Beta-blockers and diuretics

Can impair thermoregulation and provoke hypoglycaemia, especially in diabetic patients on insulin or insulin-secretagogues. Educate on heat-intolerance signs (headache, dizziness, heavy sweating, cramping, nausea) and hypoglycaemia signs (dizziness, sweating, blurred vision, tingling lips, hunger); advise proper food intake before exercising.

05 — Clinical Pathway

Suggested content of the physiotherapy program

A sequential pathway from referral to outcome measurement. Actual content may vary by local facilities and practice.

1

Documentation

Referral source and reason for referral.

2

Subjective examination

Check inclusion/exclusion criteria, exercise habit (IPAQ), barriers to exercise, stage of change in readiness of exercise, and past medical history.

3

Objective examination

Body composition (BMI, waist circumference, hip-waist ratio, body fat/lean mass); blood pressure at clinic and/or home (home BP recommended if white-coat effect suspected); physical fitness assessments — 2-Minute Step Test, 30-Second Arm Curl, 30-Second Chair Stand; MSK assessment of lower limbs if indicated.

4

Goal setting — SMART

Specific · Measurable · Adjustable · Realistic · Time-frame specific. Example: "Additional 30-minute level-ground brisk walking in the park, 3×/week for one month."

5

Action planning

Use a relapse-prevention model to identify high-risk situations (bad weather, inconvenient timing, being alone/tired, negative emotions) and build alternatives in advance (e.g. indoor stepping if it rains). Reinforce with AHA tips: personalise the activity, habitualise the routine, exercise with others, and start small with gradual progression.

6

Exercise prescription

Aerobic + resistance + flexibility/balance training, per the FITT tables above.

7

Outcome measurement

BMI, blood pressure, body weight and composition, physical fitness assessments, stage of change, Chinese Self-Efficacy for Exercise Scale (SEE-C), IPAQ, KAP questionnaire, and patient satisfaction survey.

06 — Measuring Intensity

When heart rate isn't reliable

Because beta-blockers blunt the HR response, perceived exertion and the Talk Test are essential alternatives for exercise intensity monitoring.

Heart Rate Reserve method

HRR = Max HR − Resting HR
Target HR = (HRR × %intensity) + Resting HR

Moderate intensity = 40–59% HRR (or VO₂R) · Vigorous intensity = 60–89% HRR (or VO₂R)

07 — Long-Term Adherence

Keeping the habit after the program ends

Self-efficacy is a key driver of long-term maintenance. Tools like HA Go can prescribe exercises, track performance, and give positive feedback to sustain patient confidence.

PersonaliseChoose activities that match the patient's own interests, not a generic prescription.
HabitualiseAnchor exercise to a fixed time of day so it becomes routine rather than a decision.
SocialiseExercising with family or friends improves consistency and enjoyment.
Progress graduallyStart with small, achievable bouts and build up — this also protects against BP-related risk.

01 · Why this matters

Background & definition

Cardiac disease is the third commonest cause of death in Hong Kong, accounting for 13.0% of all deaths (2020), and referrals into HA cardiac rehabilitation grow every year. Rehab is one of the few interventions proven to cut cardiac mortality and reinfarction — which is why the physiotherapist's role in it is protocol-driven, not optional.

Cardiac rehabilitation is the process by which patients, in partnership with a multidisciplinary team, are supported to optimise health and physical fitness, prevent recurrence, reduce mortality, and return to work and life roles — with partners, family and carers involved throughout.

§1.1 Definition of Cardiac Rehabilitation
Multidisciplinary

Individualised risk-profile management, dietary advice, exercise prescription, activity counselling and psychosocial support delivered as one package of care.

Goal

Optimise fitness and health, prevent recurrence, reduce mortality — and hand the patient the skills to self-manage for the long term.

Family & carers

Involvement of partners, family and carers is written into the definition itself — not an optional extra to education.

02 · The care pathway

Four phases, one continuum

Cardiac rehab in Hong Kong runs across four linked phases. Physiotherapists lead the exercise components of Phase I and II, and hand off into Phase III/IV community and long-term maintenance. Hybrid tele-components are increasingly layered across every phase.

PHASE I In-patient

Stabilise & educate

  • Medical evaluation
  • Reassurance & education
  • Correct cardiac misconceptions
  • Risk assessment
  • Discharge planning
  • Family involvement
PHASE II Out-patient, early post-discharge

Supervised rebuild

  • Structured programme
  • Supervised exercise
  • Counselling & secondary-prevention reinforcement
  • Structured empowerment / self-help programme
PHASE III Community / home

Consolidate habits

  • Psychological support
  • Menu-based approach
  • Education & training, smoking cessation
  • Regular exercise, weight management
  • Vocational rehab, peer support
PHASE IV Long-term, community

Sustain for life

  • Collaborate with NGOs, support groups, leisure centres
  • Maintain physical activity & lifestyle change
  • Telemedicine woven across phases

Hybrid / telehealth is now mainstream

Evidence supports telehealth or hybrid delivery as safe and effective alongside face-to-face rehab. Think video platforms, pedometers/accelerometers, tele-ECG, chest/wrist HR sensors, home BP monitors and AI-supported digital tools — usable across Phase I–IV, not just Phase III/IV. §1.2 & §7

Programme objectives §1.3 & §1.4

Phase I objectives
  • Identify and evaluate patient needs; prescribe an in-patient exercise programme matched to their individual condition
  • Explain, teach and supervise pre- and post-op procedures for patients requiring cardiac surgery
  • Decrease the deconditioning effects of prolonged bed rest
  • Minimise the chance of pulmonary atelectasis and thromboembolic complications
  • Enhance cardiovascular and musculoskeletal functioning
  • Promote psychological recovery from anxiety or depression
  • Decrease the period of hospitalisation
  • Prepare patients for Phase II cardiac rehabilitation
  • Prevent recurrence by identifying, stratifying and modifying risk factors
  • Promote a more independent and active lifestyle after discharge
Phase II objectives
  • Improve functional capacity after discharge from hospital
  • Control and modify cardiovascular risk factors for secondary prevention
  • Empower patients and family members to adopt a healthy lifestyle
  • Provide guidelines for physical activity and a long-term exercise programme
  • Reduce anxiety and depression, and improve quality of life

Your role as the physiotherapist §1.5 & §1.6

In Phase I
  • Identify, stratify and optimise risk factors
  • Minimise deconditioning effects due to prolonged bed rest
  • Minimise the occurrence of pulmonary complications
  • Maintain neuromuscular relaxation
  • Provide pre- and post-op assessment and training for surgical cardiac patients
  • Select suitable candidates for the Phase II programme
  • Co-ordinate the exercise programme
  • Prescribe and monitor exercise
  • Empower the patient to control modifiable risk factors
  • Explore premorbid physical activity level
  • Advise on post-discharge physical activity level
In Phase II
  • Control cardiovascular risk, global cardiovascular risk and mortality
  • Control symptoms; improve physical fitness, body composition and psychological well-being
  • Empower the patient to control modifiable risk factors
  • Establish goals of exercise training
  • Increase participation in physical activities
  • Establish a long-term exercise habit in the community, safely
03 · Screening at the door

Who's in, who waits

Before any patient enters Phase I or II, screen against the guideline's indication and contraindication lists. Contraindications are checked at every session, not just at intake — a patient can be indicated today and contraindicated tomorrow.

Indicated for Phase I & II

  • Medically stable post-MI
  • Stable angina
  • CABG surgery
  • Percutaneous coronary angioplasty (PTCA)
  • Stable heart failure (systolic or diastolic)
  • Heart transplantation
  • Valvular heart surgery
  • Peripheral arterial disease
  • At CAD risk: diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension or obesity
  • Others per physician referral & team consensus

Contraindicated — hold the session

  • Unstable angina
  • Uncontrolled hypertension (SBP>180 and/or DBP>110 mmHg)
  • Orthostatic BP drop >20 mmHg with symptoms
  • Significant aortic stenosis (valve area <1.0 cm²)
  • Uncontrolled atrial/ventricular arrhythmia or sinus tachycardia >120 bpm
  • Uncompensated heart failure
  • 3rd-degree AV block without pacemaker
  • Active pericarditis / myocarditis
  • Recent embolism, thrombophlebitis, aortic dissection
  • Acute systemic illness, fever, or uncontrolled DM
  • Severe orthopedic or uncorrected metabolic conditions
  • Severe psychological disorder
04 · Stratify before you prescribe

Risk stratification & disease staging

Use AACVPR for anyone with a cardiac diagnosis, and ACSM for apparently healthy individuals being screened for CAD risk. Risk tier drives how tightly you supervise and monitor exercise.

● Low risk

ALL of these must be true:

  • No complex ventricular dysrhythmia at rest/ on test/ recovery
  • No angina or significant symptoms
  • Normal haemodynamic response
  • Functional capacity ≥7 METs
  • LVEF ≥50%, uncomplicated MI/revasc
  • No CHF, no post-event ischaemia, no depression
Supervision (Appendix 1) Direct medical supervision 6–18 sessions or 30 days; continuous ECG tapering as tolerated.
● Moderate risk

ANY one of these:

  • Angina/symptoms only at high exertion (≥7 METs)
  • Mild–moderate silent ischaemia (ST dep <2mm)
  • Functional capacity <5 METs
  • LVEF 40–49%
Supervision (Appendix 1) Direct staff supervision 12–24 sessions or 60 days; continuous ECG tapering to intermittent/none.
● High risk

ANY one of these:

  • Complex ventricular dysrhythmia on test/recovery
  • Angina/symptoms at low exertion (<5 METs) or recovery
  • High silent ischaemia (ST dep ≥2mm)
  • Abnormal haemodynamics (chronotropic incompetence, flat/falling SBP, severe post-ex hypotension)
  • LVEF <40%, arrest history, CHF, complex resting dysrhythmia, depression
Supervision (Appendix 1) Direct staff supervision 18–36 sessions or 90 days; continuous ECG tapering to intermittent. Follow the 7-step protocol strictly — no step-jumping.

AACVPR blind spot

This algorithm does not account for comorbidities such as Type 2 diabetes, morbid obesity, severe pulmonary disease or debilitating neuro/orthopaedic conditions — use clinical judgement to add caution on top. §4.1.3 note

New ACSM recommendations — exercise testing before exercise §4.3

Recommend medical exercise testing prior to starting exercise in any of the following:

  • Unstable, new or possible symptoms of cardiovascular disease
  • End-stage renal disease
  • Symptomatic or diagnosed pulmonary disease — COPD, asthma, interstitial lung disease or cystic fibrosis
  • Diabetes mellitus plus at least one of:
  • Age >35 years
  • Type 2 diabetes >10-year duration
  • Type 1 diabetes >15-year duration
  • Hypercholesterolaemia (total cholesterol ≥240 mg·L⁻¹ / 6.62 mmol·L⁻¹)
  • Hypertension (SBP ≥140 or DBP ≥90 mmHg)
  • Smoking
  • Family history of CAD in a first-degree relative <60 years
  • Presence of microvascular disease
  • Peripheral artery disease
  • Autonomic neuropathy

05 · Ward-based rehabilitation

Phase I: the in-patient programme

Before mobilising, confirm the AACVPR in-patient readiness parameters. Then progress through self-care, active/passive ROM and early ambulation, monitored tightly against fixed HR, RPE and symptom ceilings.

The first 48 hours after MI or cardiac surgery §5

Activity in this window is restricted to self-care activities, arm and leg range of movement, and postural change — nothing more. Simple exposure to orthostatic or gravitational stress, such as intermittent sitting or standing during hospital convalescence, prevents much of the deterioration in exercise performance that follows an acute cardiac event. A structured, formalised in-hospital exercise programme with ward activities included is beneficial after acute MI or cardiac surgery.

Ready to mobilise? — AACVPR in-patient parameters

  • No new or recurrent chest pain in the previous 8 hours
  • Stable or falling creatine kinase and troponin values
  • No sign of decompensated heart failure (e.g. resting dyspnoea, bibasilar rales)
  • Normal cardiac rhythm and stable ECG

General physiotherapy assessment §5.2

History & baseline
  • Personal particulars
  • Current medical history and condition
  • Past medical history
  • Social and economic history, including risk factor identification
  • Mobility level before admission
Examination & records
  • General condition and vital signs
  • Physical assessment — cardiopulmonary, neurological and musculoskeletal systems
  • Cardiac-specific investigations: cardiac catheterisation and echocardiogram reports, ECG, cardiac enzyme trend and other laboratory results, chest X-ray, CT coronary angiogram, stress test report
  • Responses and progression throughout the programme
  • Physiotherapist's comment / recommendation

General exercise guidelines §5.3

Frequency
2–4 mobilisation sessions/day for the first 3 days of stay.
Intensity
Post-MI: HRrest + 20 bpm. Post-surgery: HRrest + 30 bpm. Hard ceiling ≤120 bpm and RPE ≤13 (6–20 scale).
Duration
Intermittent walking bouts of 3–5 min, building up; rest periods shorter than work — aim for a 2:1 exercise:rest ratio.
Type
Walking.
Progression
Once continuous 10–15 min is tolerated, increase intensity within RPE/HR limits.

Stop exercise if the patient reports

  • Chest pain or discomfort
  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Fatigue or dizziness
  • Cold sweating
  • Muscle cramp

House rules

  • BP / Pulse should be recorded in position correlate to the activity before and after exercise
  • Pulse should not rise >20 bpm after exercise
  • Always warm up and cool down
  • TNG on standby if prescribed
  • Caution with fixed-rate (non-rate-responsive) pacemakers
  • Avoid: post-meal (<1h), when upset, temperature extremes, isometric work, small-muscle work, over-fatigue

Surgical cardiac patients — day-by-day §5.4

Pre-operative

Assess chest & musculoskeletal status, baseline 6MWT if applicable. Teach breathing exercises, wound-support coughing, active limb exercises (if tolerated), ankle pumps.

Admission

Assess: chest condition, musculoskeletal system, and exercise tolerance — if it was not done pre-operatively, or if exercise tolerance has since changed.

Re-teach: breathing exercises, wound-support coughing, active limb exercises and ankle pumps, so the patient can already perform them before going to theatre.

Operative day

Monitor vitals, drainage and ventilator status; treat on request only.

POD 1 (after extubation)

  • Bedside Assessment including vital signs, chest and musculoskeletal system
  • Bilateral lower-costal & diaphragmatic breathing, wound-support coughing
  • Patient can receive treatment in alternate side-lying position
  • Incentive spirometry, active limb exercises, transfers, ankle pumps
  • Sit out of bed ± cycling if stable
  • No chest physiotherapy if the sternum has not closed — passive limb ROM only, with caution

POD 2

Add vasomotor training, encourage sitting out, standing/stepping, and walking if haemodynamically stable.

POD 3

Continue walking; address postural awareness — watch for post-op kyphosis from wound-pain guarding.

POD 4

Stair-walking training if haemodynamically stable and asymptomatic.

POD 5

Home exercise and ADL advice; select suitable candidates for Phase II.

Sternotomy wound care essentials

  • Check for oozing, swelling, erythema; assess wound pain before exercising
  • Cough with the wound supported; secure drains/lines with enough slack to move
  • Resume trunk/shoulder ROM early; unilateral exercise if pain is severe
  • Avoid moderate–heavy resistance training for 3 months post-op
  • Use sustained-hold technique, not bouncing, to ease muscle guarding
  • Lifting limit ~5–10 lb (<50% MVC) for 10–12 weeks; keep an upright posture in 10-12 weeks
  • Most patient can achieve full painfree AROM of UL in 6weeks
  • Patient should keep good upright posture

Medical cardiac patients — how the programme runs §5.5

The Phase I exercise programme begins at a low level of exertion on a daily basis, around 5–20 minutes for post-AMI patients, and continues in a stepwise fashion throughout the hospitalisation period. The patient is advanced through a series of passive, active and resisted exercises in supine, sitting and upright positions. Most patients are able to begin with active exercises, although passive exercises are occasionally warranted for those with extensive myocardial damage or other complications. Under normal circumstances the patient is intensively monitored during exercise and their progress reviewed regularly.

Principles of exercise prescription §5.5.1
  • Low-intensity physical activities
  • Active or passive range of movement of all limbs and trunk
  • Isotonic rather than isometric exercise, using large muscle groups
  • Early ambulation
  • Avoid pushing the patient to maximum effort at one time
  • Use the 7-step exercise programme — exercises divided into steps for the patient to progress through
Monitoring §5.5.2

Before exercise, and before advancing to a higher level, evaluate pulse, blood pressure, ECG waveform and rhythm, appearance and symptoms.

  • Assess BP, HR, ECG and RPE before, during and after exercise
  • HR <120 bpm, or HRrest + 20 bpm (arbitrary upper limit)
  • RPE <13 on the 6–20 Borg scale

Jumping steps?

Allowed only for Low/Moderate-risk patients, and only while HR, BP, ECG, RPE and symptoms all stay within limits (see below). High-risk patients must follow all 7 steps in strict order.

Criteria to allow jumping a step

Patient exceeds the current step already, is Low/Moderate risk, and none of the following are present:

  • HR ≥120 bpm or ≥ HRrest+20
  • SBP >180 & DBP >100 mmHg, or SBP fails to rise / rises <20 mmHg
  • Abnormal ECG changes (ST shift >2mm, septal Q wave, AV block, arrhythmia)
  • RPE ≥13
  • Angina, nausea, dyspnoea, dizziness, unsteadiness, cyanosis, confusion, cold sweating

Discontinue exercise in Phase I if

  • Diastolic BP ≥110 mmHg
  • Systolic BP drops >10 mmHg with increasing workload
  • Significant ventricular/atrial dysrhythmia, with or without symptoms
  • 2nd- or 3rd-degree heart block
  • Angina, marked dyspnoea, or ischaemic ECG changes
06 · Outpatient reconditioning

Phase II: building capacity safely

Phase II opens with objective assessment, then applies structured FITT prescriptions for aerobic and resistance training — adjusted for sternotomy, diabetes and weight-management goals.

Assessment before you prescribe §6.1

6-Minute Walk Test

Self-paced walking capacity over a flat 30m+ course, using standardised encouragement phrases every 60s. Distance walked is the primary outcome.

Submaximal exercise test

Treadmill preferred; cycle ergometry for obesity/orthopaedic/vascular/neuro limitations (expect 5–20% lower peak capacity). Target test duration 8–12 min. Terminate at 70% heart-rate reserve (≈85% age-predicted max HR) even without symptoms.

Protocols with small incremental increase (e.g. Balke-Ware ≤1MET per stage) are preferred.

Absolute contraindications to testing

  • MI within 2 days; ongoing unstable angina
  • Uncontrolled arrhythmia with haemodynamic compromise
  • Active endocarditis, myo/pericarditis
  • Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis; decompensated HF
  • Acute PE, pulmonary infarction, DVT, aortic dissection
  • Physical disability precluding safe testing

Relative contraindications to testing

  • Known left main coronary stenosis
  • Moderate–severe AS of uncertain symptom relationship
  • Uncontrolled tachyarrhythmia; advanced/complete heart block
  • Recent stroke/TIA; limited ability to cooperate
  • Resting SBP >200 or DBP >110 mmHg
  • Uncorrected anaemia, electrolyte imbalance, hyperthyroidism

Running a submaximal exercise test §6.1.1.2.2

Pre-test instructions & preparation
  • Wear loose-fitting, comfortable clothes suitable for exercise
  • Avoid food, alcohol and caffeine for at least 3 hours beforehand
  • Avoid strenuous exercise on the day of the test
  • Sleep at least 6–8 hours the night before
  • Give a detailed explanation of the procedure, and instruct the patient to give maximal exertion so the data is usable
  • Take informed consent covering purpose, expectations and risks
  • Record usual medications — some affect exercise performance, haemodynamics and the ECG
Setting up
  • Record a resting supine 12-lead ECG and BP before exercise, then repeat supine and standing (sitting if using a cycle ergometer)
  • Skin prep: shave electrode sites, then rub with alcohol-saturated gauze. Disposable electrodes are generally silver–silver chloride with adherent gel
  • Treadmill: discourage tight grasping of the front or side rails — one finger of each hand on the rail for balance, once the patient is accustomed to walking
  • Cycle ergometer: upright posture, five-degree bend at the knee at maximal leg extension, hands correctly positioned on the handlebars
During the test — the sequence
  • Begin with a 2–3 minute low-workload warm-up to acquaint the patient with the equipment and prepare them for stage-one intensity
  • Select a protocol giving a total duration of 8–12 minutes, with loads increasing progressively at the designated interval for each stage
  • Monitor the 12-lead ECG continuously; record during the last 5–10 seconds of each stage
  • Monitor HR continuously; record during the last 5–10 seconds of each minute
  • Record BP in the last 30–60 seconds of each stage, and repeat it if there is a hypotensive or hypertensive response
  • Record RPE and any additional scales (angina or dyspnoea) during the last 5–10 seconds of each stage; take peak RPE shortly after the test is terminated
  • Monitor the patient's appearance and symptoms regularly throughout
  • Terminate at 70% heart rate reserve (≈85% of age-predicted maximal HR) — or earlier if the patient fails to conform to the protocol, develops adverse signs or symptoms, requests to stop, or an emergency occurs
  • Cool down by continuing to exercise at the first-stage work rate or lower — or use a passive cool-down if the patient has signs of discomfort or an emergency arises
  • Recover for at least 6 minutes. Record ECG and BP immediately post-exercise, again at 60 seconds, then every 2 minutes; HR is monitored continuously and recorded in the last 5–10 seconds of each minute. Continue low-level exercise until HR and BP stabilise — they need not return to pre-exercise levels. Extend the post-test surveillance period if light-headedness, angina, claudication or unusual fatigue/dyspnoea appear

When to terminate the test §6.1.1.2.3

Absolute indications — stop now

  • ST elevation >1.0 mm in leads without pre-existing Q waves from prior MI (other than aVR, aVL or V₁)
  • Drop in SBP ≥10 mmHg despite an increase in workload, when accompanied by other evidence of ischaemia
  • Moderate-to-severe angina
  • Central nervous system symptoms — ataxia, dizziness or near syncope
  • Signs of poor perfusion (cyanosis or pallor)
  • Technical difficulty monitoring the ECG or SBP
  • The individual requests to stop
  • Sustained VT, or another arrhythmia including 2nd- or 3rd-degree AV block, that interferes with normal maintenance of cardiac output during exercise

Relative indications — weigh up and usually stop

  • Marked ST displacement (horizontal or downsloping >2 mm, measured 60–80 ms after the J point) in a patient with suspected ischaemia
  • Drop in SBP >10 mmHg persistently below baseline despite increasing work rate, without other evidence of ischaemia
  • Arrhythmias other than sustained VT — multifocal ectopy, ventricular triplets, SVT, and bradyarrhythmias that could become more complex or destabilise haemodynamics
  • Fatigue, shortness of breath, wheezing, leg cramps or claudication
  • Bundle-branch block that cannot be distinguished from ventricular tachycardia
  • Increasing chest pain
  • Exaggerated hypertensive response — SBP >250 mmHg and/or DBP >115 mmHg
  • SpO₂ ≤80%

Cardiac Exercise Self-Efficacy Instrument (CESEI-C)

A validated 16-item, 5-point Likert questionnaire (score range 16–80) measuring a patient's confidence to exercise safely, correctly, at the right dose and intensity — useful alongside physical outcome measures to track readiness for self-management. §6.1.2, Appendix 4

Principles of exercise training §6.2.1

Overload

Train above habitual load — manipulate load, reps, speed, volume or rest.

Specificity

Adaptations match the muscles, movement pattern, energy system and intensity trained.

Individual differences

Response varies with baseline fitness — prescribe to the person, not the protocol.

Reversibility

Detraining starts within 1–2 weeks; most gains fade within months of stopping.

Aerobic exercise prescription §6.3

Intensity
40–80% of exercise capacity (HRR / VO₂R / VO₂peak); or HRrest+20–30 bpm if untested; RPE 12–16 (6–20 scale). Keep ≥10 bpm below any known ischaemic threshold.
Frequency
≥3 days/week, preferably ≥5 days/week.
Duration
5–10 min warm-up and cool-down; 20–60 min conditioning phase. Start from as little as 5–10 min and progress 10–20%/week.
Type
Rhythmic, large-muscle-group activity — arm/leg ergometers, treadmill, cycles, stepper, rower, elliptical.
Progression
No fixed formula — individualise to tolerance, fitness, motivation and goals, symptoms and musculoskeletal limits.

Resistance training prescription §6.4

Eligibility & timing

Low–moderate risk (high risk only with supervision). Start ≥5 weeks post-MI/surgery (with 4 weeks of consistent endurance training first), or ≥2–3 weeks post-PTCA (2 weeks consistent endurance training). ROM and very light 1–3 lb loads may start earlier if tolerated. No CHF, uncontrolled dysrhythmia, severe valvular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or unstable symptoms.

Intensity
10–15 reps without straining (~30–40% 1RM upper body, ~50–60% lower body); low-risk patients may progress to 8–12 reps at 60–80% 1RM. RPE 11–14 as a subjective guide.
Time
1 set/major muscle group initially, 8–10 exercises, 1–3 sets; large muscles before small; include multi-joint "compound" exercises.
Frequency
2–3 non-consecutive days/week, ≥48h between sessions for the same muscle group; after the aerobic component, once warmed up.
Type
Elastic bands; cuff and hand weights; free weights; wall pulleys; machines (effective load depends on lever-arm weight and range of motion).
Progression
~2–5 lb/week upper body, ~5–10 lb/week lower body — or add reps/sets/training days instead of load.
Equipment §6.4.1
  • Elastic bands
  • Cuff and hand weights
  • Free weights
  • Wall pulleys
  • Machines — remember the resistance felt depends on the weight of the lever arms and the range of motion

A resistance-training programme is defined as one in which patients lift ≥50% of 1-RM. Elastic bands, 1–3 lb (0.45–1.36 kg) hand weights and light free weights may be introduced progressively at outpatient programme entry provided no other contraindications exist.

Proper technique §6.4.1
  • Raise and lower weights with slow, controlled movements, to full extension
  • Maintain a regular breathing pattern — no breath holding or straining (Valsalva manoeuvre)
  • Avoid straining
  • Avoid sustained, tight gripping, which can evoke an excessive blood pressure response
  • Use RPE 11–14 ("light" to "somewhat hard" on the 6–20 scale) as a subjective guide to effort
  • Terminate the exercise if warning signs or symptoms appear — dizziness, arrhythmias, unusual shortness of breath or angina

Sternotomy resistance precautions §6.4.2

  • <6 weeks: minimise UL load, no lifting >5 lb, no bench press / pec fly / lat pulldown; LL training unaffected
  • Sternal instability reported in up to 16% of cases even in uncomplicated healing (typical union 8–10 weeks)
  • 5–6 to <12 weeks: pain-free unloaded UL ROM; lifting ≤5–10 lb (<50% MVC)
  • >12 weeks: progressive gentle UL resistance as tolerated

Cap intensity if you see §6.4.3

  • Angina or other cardiac-insufficiency symptoms
  • SBP plateau/drop >10 mmHg, SBP >240, or DBP ≥110 mmHg
  • 1mm ST depression, horizontal or downsloping
  • Radionuclide evidence of left ventricular dysfunction
  • Increasing ventricular arrhythmia frequency, or other significant ECG change

Keep peak exercise HR ≥10 bpm below whatever HR triggered these findings.

Exercise for patients with diabetes §6.5

Frequency
3–7 days/week.
Intensity
40–59% VO₂R/HRR (RPE 11–12); better glycaemic control possible at 60–89% VO₂R (RPE 14–17) once regularly active.
Time
≥150 min/week moderate-or-greater intensity; extra benefit accrues to ≥300 min/week.
Type
Large-muscle, rhythmic, continuous activity matched to patient interest. Resistance training also encouraged unless contraindicated.
Progression
Gradual progression of both intensity and volume is recommended. Aim at maximizing caloric expenditure.
Complication-specific cautions
  • Retinopathy: avoid BP spikes, vigorous/jumping/jarring, head-down positions, Valsalva
  • Autonomic neuropathy: HR/BP may be blunted — lean on RPE; watch for silent ischaemia (unusual SOB/back pain)
  • Peripheral neuropathy: proactive foot care to prevent ulcers

Pre-exercise glucose protocol

  • ≤100 mg·dL⁻¹ → delay, give up to 15g carbohydrate, re-test in 15 min
  • ≥250 mg·dL⁻¹ → exercise only if well, hydrated and ketone-negative; monitor closely
  • ≥350 mg·dL⁻¹ → correct with insulin before exercising (for insulin users), even without ketones
  • Ensure euhydration and take H'stix before exercise
  • Avoid exercising at peak insulin action; inject away from the muscle being exercised
  • Caution: delayed hypoglycaemia possible up to 12h after exercise

Exercise for weight management §6.6

Prevent weight gain
150–250 min/week moderate-intensity activity
Weight loss
150–225 min/week gives modest loss; >250 min/week gives clinically significant loss
Maintain loss
>250–300 min/week moderate-to-vigorous activity helps prevent regain
ACSM baseline
≥150 min/week (30 min × 5 days) as a starting target, plus strength training; diet + activity beats diet alone
07 · Beyond the gym session

Patient empowerment & self-management

Every Phase I and II contact is also a teaching contact. Build these into routine sessions rather than a single discharge talk.

Teach every patient
  • What the CR team does and why exercise helps their heart
  • How to self-monitor with RPE and heart rate
  • Smoking cessation, and a diet low in salt, sugar, cholesterol, saturated and trans-fat
  • Stress management
  • Safe ADL, household, occupational and recreational activity levels
  • A home exercise plan with clear precautions

Warning signs — report to a doctor immediately

  • Chest pain radiating to neck/jaw, not relieved by TNG
  • Resting palpitations or shortness of breath
  • Dizziness or excessive fatigue
  • Nausea, vomiting, or headache
  • Swelling of the extremities
  • Cold sweating or cyanosis

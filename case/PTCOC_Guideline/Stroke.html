Manual on Physiotherapy Management in Stroke Rehabilitation

A working summary of the HA manual for orientation and self-study — covering the stroke care pathway, assessment, motor recovery, complication prevention, discharge planning and outcome measures, finishing with a case-study quiz.

Source: HA Head Office, Specialty Group on Neurological & Gerontology Version 2.0 · Effective 16/12/2025 Review due: 16/12/2028

Overview

Why stroke rehabilitation matters in Hong Kong, the hospital-to-community care pathway, and how to read the evidence grading used throughout this manual and this summary.

Scope

Stroke (cerebrovascular accident, CVA) is an acute disturbance of focal or global cerebral function lasting >24 hours or leading to death, presumably of vascular origin (WHO, 1989). The manual applies to physiotherapy management of stroke patients across the full HA care pathway — acute hospital care through to community reintegration.

4th
leading cause of death in Hong Kong, after cancer, pneumonia & ischaemic heart disease
>3,000
deaths per year in Hong Kong from stroke (CHP, 2023)
~24,000
stroke patients admitted to HA hospitals annually (HA Statistical Report, 2016/17)

Care pathway

Physiotherapy spans the full journey from hospital to community. Telehealth rehabilitation is offered whenever indicated at any stage.

Stroke Patient → Acute Care → Extended Care → Home / Institution → Day Rehab / OPD / Community Services
Timing standard:

Per the Acute Ischaemic Stroke Care Guideline (HAHO, 2017), physiotherapists should assess stroke patients within one working day of admission and provide supportive treatment in an Acute Stroke Unit. During this initial phase, the priority is stabilisation of the patient's medical/neurological condition and prevention of secondary complications such as limb contracture and chest complications.

Once medically stable, the physiotherapist identifies and prioritises problems across six domains: motor impairment, sensory & perceptual impairment, psychological impairment, cognitive impairment, communication impairment, and risk of developing other complications — with the sensori-motor, mobility and functional domains generally given the most emphasis.

Vital signs & labs:

Physiotherapists should monitor vital signs and laboratory results, since these affect physical performance and guide precautions during intervention.

Emerging assistive technologies noted in the manual

Body weight-supported treadmill training (BWSTT), passive gait simulators, robotic-assisted therapy, computerised balance training with force sensors, virtual reality, and non-invasive brain stimulation (tDCS, rTMS) may target more than one impairment domain at once. Clinical judgement is needed for their application across different rehabilitation settings, taking resource availability into account.

Motor Recovery: Principles

The rationale behind training design, and the prognostic indicators that shape expectations for upper-limb recovery.

Why training is designed the way it is

Motor recovery relates to neuroplasticity — dendrite sprouting, new synapse formation and alteration of existing synapses. Most rehabilitation protocols are therefore grounded in motor learning theory: functional gain is greater when training is meaningful, repetitive and intensive, and different combinations of treatment approach can produce similar functional gains. Two design principles follow from this:

Task-specific training 1a

A meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies found task-specific training produced functional gain accompanied by neural changes in the sensorimotor cortex of the affected hemisphere. 1b2 Repetitive task-specific training is also effective for lower-limb motor performance, sit-to-stand performance and gait.

Enriched environment

An environment offering greater opportunity for physical activity and motivation. Animal studies show training in an enriched environment produces greater motor recovery with enhanced neural plasticity.

Upper limb recovery — prognostic indicators

~80%
of stroke patients report significant hemiplegic UL impairment
<15%
of post-stroke patients restore normal UL function in daily activities
60%
with active finger extension within 72h regain full UL function (ARAT) at 6 months
  • Active finger extension is a strong predictor of short, medium and long-term recovery.
  • Minimal shoulder abduction and proximal (upper) motor control at admission gives a reasonable chance of regaining hand capacity; patients without proximal arm control have a poor prognosis for regaining hand capacity — shoulder abduction control is therefore another useful early indicator.

Constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) — know the candidacy criteria

1a A meta-analysis found CIMT, EMG biofeedback and robotic therapy show potential effect on hemiplegic arm recovery. CIMT's effect is relatively robust, but applicability is limited — traditional CIMT targets patients with limited arm impairment who can tolerate prolonged constraint:

ProtocolNon-paretic limb constraintTask-oriented training
Traditional CIMTUp to 90% of waking hours, for 2–3 weeksUp to 6 hours/day
Low-intensity CIMT 1a<90% of waking hours<3 hours/day

Low-intensity CIMT: meta-analysis of 23 RCTs, 627 subjects, positive effect on arm-hand activities (Veerbeek et al., 2014).

Moderate-quality evidence bundle (Cochrane overview):

CIMT, mental practice, mirror therapy, interventions for sensory impairment, virtual reality, and a relatively high dose of repetitive task practice all show a beneficial effect on upper limb function after stroke.

Mobility & balance training — dosage and evidence

  • 1a Early balance training — at least 20–60 minutes, 3–4 times a week, for 6–12 weeks after stroke.
  • 1a Repetitive task-oriented training improves sit-to-stand performance (7 trials, 346 participants), and walking speed, functional ambulation and walking distance (Cochrane review, 14 trials, 658 participants).
  • 1a Gait-oriented physical training (3 systematic reviews) improves gait speed and endurance, and reduces the level of assistance needed during walking.
  • 1a Force-plate biofeedback for standing balance (4 trials, 161 participants).
  • 1b Stair gait training with proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (30 min); gait trainer with partial body-weight support plus overground gait training; walking exercise on stairs enhances balance.
  • 1b Individual trials also support hydrotherapy, the Bobath approach, and Tai Chi.

Motor Recovery: Interventions

Technology-assisted and adjunct modalities for motor recovery, with the strength and limits of the current evidence.

Robotic training

Robot-assisted therapy (RAT) delivers precise, repetitive, task-specific, high-dosage training while reducing manpower burden.

Upper limb 1

Common devices: ArmeoPower, MIT-Manus, Hand of Hope. A 2025 review of 16 meta-analyses (396 RCTs, 19,280 patients) found RAT significantly enhances UL motor function and strength, standalone or alongside conventional therapy at the same intensity, consistently across patient groups, devices and treatment duration.

Gait 1

Exoskeletons (e.g. Lokomat, EksoGT, ReWalk) and end-effector robots (e.g. G-EO System, Gait Trainer) show comparable effects in subacute patients (13 RCTs, 673 participants). Robot-assisted gait training (RAGT) + conventional therapy beats either alone; earlier initiation gives better recovery. Evidence for biomechanical effects (gait speed, step length, asymmetry) remains very low quality.

Balance 1

Exoskeleton RAT gave significantly greater Berg Balance Scale gains than conventional therapy in acute/subacute stroke (13 RCTs, 517 participants) — but no significant benefit over conventional therapy was shown in chronic stroke.

Electrical stimulation

Functional electrical stimulation (FES) replaces/assists voluntary contraction during a functional task; cyclical stimulation targets a muscle repeatedly for strengthening. 3 Effectiveness depends heavily on target muscles, stroke severity, timing post-stroke, and treatment dose/duration/mode.

Upper limb 1

FES-based rehabilitation (25 studies) significantly improved Fugl-Meyer (FMA) and Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) scores. EMG-controlled closed-loop FES gave the largest effect (FMA mean difference up to 14.14 points, ARAT mean difference 11.9 points, p<0.001).

Lower limb / gait — chronic-phase dosing

1 FES alone did not significantly improve gait speed but did improve ankle dorsiflexion, balance and functional mobility; FES + supervised exercise gave greater gait-speed gains. NMES appears more effective in the chronic phase, and shorter sessions of ~20–30 minutes gave better outcomes than longer sessions, which may raise the neural excitability threshold and cause adaptation.

1 Cyclical electrical stimulation (16 studies) increases muscle strength and activity performance with carryover effect, but is not shown to be better than other strengthening interventions, and optimal dosage remains unclear.

Acupuncture

An HA operational guideline for physiotherapists on acupuncture practice has been in place since 2009, reviewed every three years.

  • 2 Additional acupuncture on top of conventional treatment gives extra motor-recovery benefit in sub-acute stroke; electroacupuncture enhances corticospinal tract integrity (similar motor gains between groups but greater plasticity, reduced degeneration).
  • 1 A review of 88 studies (6,431 participants) found acupuncture + conventional rehab reduces post-stroke spasticity, with benefit increasing at higher treatment frequency.
  • 1 A meta-analysis of 40 trials (2,554 patients) found acupuncture + rehabilitation outperformed rehabilitation alone for hemiplegic shoulder pain, motor function, ADL and shoulder ROM, with no significant adverse effects.
Virtual reality (VR)

Immersive VR (head-mounted display/large projection) gives a stronger sense of presence; non-immersive VR (standard screens) is more accessible for clinical use.

  • VR training is more effective than conventional therapy for upper-limb function; combining haptic gloves + VR with conventional therapy gives significantly greater gains than conventional therapy alone.
  • VR offers superior balance recovery vs conventional therapy — more effective in the chronic phase than the acute phase — and significantly enhances walking speed and cadence.
Non-invasive brain stimulation

rTMS

Based on the interhemispheric inhibition model: high-frequency (>1Hz) rTMS boosts excitability in the lesioned hemisphere; low-frequency (≤1Hz) rTMS suppresses the overactive contralesional hemisphere. Clinical results are inconsistent overall, but rTMS may be particularly effective in patients with a motor-evoked potential (MEP) response, subcortical lesions, mild paresis and preserved baseline function.

tDCS

A weak constant current (typically 1–2 mA) via scalp electrodes; anodal stimulation generally increases cortical excitability, cathodal decreases it. Evidence is heterogeneous, but tDCS may promote adaptive plasticity with positive pre-/post-motor rehabilitation effects, and anodal tDCS may help paretic UL motor function in chronic stroke.

Sensory, Cognitive & Mood

Non-motor impairments that shape how — and whether — a patient can engage with training, and the physiotherapist's role in each.

Sensory impairment

up to 85%
of stroke survivors have some somatic sensory deficit
94% / 89%
report loss of touch / loss of proprioception
64%
also have sensory deficits in the ipsilesional upper limb

Bilateral proprioceptive deficits (including at the ankle) are significant predictors of balance dysfunction. High-quality trial evidence is lacking overall (meta-analysis not possible), but individual studies and reviews support repetitive thermal stimulation, mirror therapy, perceptual-learning based sensory discrimination programmes, and multisensory integration (MSI) as low-risk, plausibly effective approaches — evidence quality is generally low-to-moderate.

Perceptual impairment — unilateral neglect

Estimated prevalence of spatial neglect after unilateral stroke is approximately 30%, higher after right brain damage. There is no systematic review or meta-analysis supporting a specific treatment approach, but individual trials show positive effects for electrical stimulation, vibration therapy, task-based/magnified mirror therapy, and multisensory integration. Prism adaptation treatment (PAT) is a promising intervention — more sessions predict greater improvement and may reduce in-hospital falls in patients with neglect. A recent Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute interventions for perceptual disorders overall — more high-quality trials are needed.

Psychological impairment

Diagnosis is difficult, particularly with speech disturbance. 1a A meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (432 CBT patients vs 385 controls) showed cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) significantly improves depression severity and quality of life, with no significant difference in cognitive function.

PT role: general psychological support to patient and caregivers (information about the illness, support and encouragement) and referral to doctor / clinical psychologist / support groups as needed.

Communication impairment

Dysarthria, apraxia and aphasia may follow motor or cognitive impairment. Strategies to enhance treatment effect:

  • Eliminate background noise; ensure the patient's attention before communicating
  • Encourage all modes of communication — speech, writing, drawing, yes/no responses
  • Allow reasonable time to respond; accept all communication attempts
  • Use simple, adult communication; simplify sentence structure and reduce speech rate
  • Emphasise key words; augment speech with gesture and visual aids; repeat when necessary

Cognitive impairment

Neglect, inattention, apraxia/dyspraxia, and difficulties with attention, memory and executive function may co-occur and adversely affect participation in therapy and ADL. Little is known about prevalence, but it is associated with slower rehabilitation progress.

PT role: screen for cognitive impairment as soon as practicable, explain impact to patient/caregivers/staff, and refer to neuropsychological expertise when appropriate. Address safety risk from persisting cognitive impairment during discharge planning.

  • Improve motivation to engage in rehabilitation
  • Minimise attentional demand — shortened intervals, rest breaks, a less distracting environment
  • Use internal/external strategies via verbal, visual or tactile input routes
  • Use compensatory techniques, e.g. checklists, to support daily performance
  • Discuss findings with patient, family, staff and others involved

Mobility Aids

Prescribing walking aids and wheelchairs — fitting, training and the evidence behind common choices.

Walking aids

Common aids: walking stick, quadripod, walking frame, rollator, and newer one-arm motorised gait devices. Choice depends on the patient's ability and walking environment.

  • Quad canes may decrease postural sway more effectively than standard canes during stance in moderate hemiparesis.
  • Cane length fitted at the greater trochanter may give more normal gait of the affected limb than wrist-crease fitting — evidence suggests greater-trochanter fitting may be preferable.
  • Cane use improves walking speed only while walking with the cane — benefits are not maintained when walking without it.
  • One-arm motorised gait devices may be more effective than parallel bars/hemi-walkers for chronic hemiplegic stroke survivors.
  • Walking aids do not correct the asymmetrical weight-bearing pattern characteristic of hemiparesis.
  • Incorrect use of walking frames is common and associated with reduced stability — guidance to patients needs to address environmental constraints.
  • Exercise-based fall-prevention interventions may reduce fall rate; evidence for other interventions is limited.

Preparation

  • Assess sensorimotor function for potential walking ability
  • Train with the proper aid across different walking environments
  • Caregiver training if assisted walking is required
  • Fall-prevention education
  • Provide aid maintenance information

Measurement

Height measured to the wrist crease or greater trochanter — evidence suggests greater-trochanter fitting may be preferable. Practice walking with the aid to fine-tune the height.

Wheelchair prescription

Recommended when a patient cannot walk due to motor impairment, cognitive impairment or decline in physical function. Common types: standard, transit, one-arm-drive (self-propulsion); electric wheelchair when necessary.

Preparation

  • Assess needs & accessibility (indoor/outdoor)
  • Caregiver training on manipulation across environments
  • Self-propelling users should have no major cognitive/spatial deficits
  • Educate transfer technique & pressure-relief
  • Supply maintenance instructions

Measurement

  • Seat width
  • Seat depth
  • Seat height
  • Armrest height
  • Seat-back height

Selection

  • Wheel type (solid/air)
  • Footrest (fixed/detachable)

  • Accessories per patient need

Complications

Complications reduce survival, hinder rehabilitation potential and lengthen hospitalisation. Patients at risk: low consciousness, multiple comorbidities, severe mobility impairment.

Common complications:

Falls, pressure sore, urinary tract infection, chest infection, depression, hemiplegic shoulder pain/subluxation, deep vein thrombosis, limb oedema, limb contracture, and pulmonary embolism.

Fall prevention

>50%
of patients report a fall within 6 months of stroke
25–73%
fall incidence range across different stroke stages
~7%
fall in the first week, acute stage
  • Falls most commonly occur during transfer from sitting to standing, or the reverse.
  • A patient's first fall is significantly associated with visuospatial neglect; repeated falls are related to age >65.
  • Balance and motion-related measures are the most common and significant fall risk factors — use these to guide fall-risk assessment.
  • Exercise appears to reduce the rate of falls, but not the number of people who fall post-stroke. Dual-task training was shown locally to be more effective in improving dual-task mobility and reducing falls/fall-related injuries in ambulatory patients with chronic stroke.
Honest evidence gap:

No single programme has currently been shown to be effective in reducing falls in people post-stroke — combine risk-factor identification with individualised balance and dual-task training rather than relying on one standard protocol.

Chest complications

Common in patients with low conscious level and poor mobility. Weakening of cough reflex and impaired swallowing are linked to aspiration pneumonia; a strong cough is protective and can help predict pneumonia risk. Chest physiotherapy — combining chest mobilisation with breathing exercises — improves respiratory muscle mobility and endurance, stabilises the trunk, and enhances balance and weight transfer.

Pressure sore

Risk: comatose, severely paralysed, obese, incontinent, or spastic patients; most common sites are the buttock and heels. Per SIGN (2010): pressure sores should not develop due to immobility after stroke if resources and expertise are adequate.

Preventive measures

  • Identify high-risk patients
  • Evaluate skin at regular intervals
  • Appropriate bed/chair equipment
  • Change position regularly
  • Ensure mobility

Interventions

  • Early mobilisation
  • Positioning
  • Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES)

Limb oedema & deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Common with decreased mobility. Early mobilisation is effective for prevention.

Preventive measures

  • Assess for signs of DVT
  • Early mobilisation — active and passive
  • Educate caregivers on limb mobilisation
  • Intermittent pneumatic compression therapy

Intervention

Compression stockings to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome in patients with proximal DVT, after anticoagulation therapy.

Limb spasticity & contracture

Spasticity — velocity-dependent hyperexcitability to stretch, with exaggerated tendon reflex, increased resistance to passive movement and hypertonia — is an independent risk factor for post-stroke pain; stretching a spastic contracted muscle can disrupt muscle fibres and excite nociceptors, producing nociceptive pain. Untreated spasticity predisposes to contracture, which impedes further rehabilitation.

Not recommended:

Splinting is not recommended (SIGN, 2010) — existing RCTs show no additional benefit.

Preventive measures

  • Proper positioning
  • Limb mobilisation
  • Stretching programme
  • Caregiver education
  • Passive standing (tilt-table / standing wheelchair)

Interventions

TENS, ultrasound and paraffin therapy give equal benefit for spasticity management. Same measures as prevention, with precaution on the joint's available range of motion.

Preventive positioning & handling

Careful positioning in the early phase minimises subluxation and later contracture, and promotes motor recovery. Release the hemiplegic arm's uninhibited pattern (adducted, internally rotated shoulder; flexed elbow; pronated forearm; flexed wrist/fingers) by retraining scapular elevation/retraction, shoulder abduction/external rotation, elbow extension, forearm supination and wrist/finger extension. Change position every 2–3 hours to prevent complications of prolonged immobilisation.

Caution:

Avoid horizontal adduction of the hemiplegic shoulder when side-lying on the unaffected side — it can traction the supra-scapular structures and cause pain.

1b A 30-minute positioning programme in maximal comfortable shoulder external rotation and 90° flexion significantly reduced the development of contracture.

1a Electrical stimulation to the hemiplegic supraspinatus and deltoid can help restore alignment of the humeral head to the glenoid fossa. Kinesio Taping is a plausible option for realigning trunk/scapula/shoulder position but lacks clinical trial evidence 3.

Not recommended:

Current evidence does not support the use of strapping or a shoulder sling for managing hemiplegic shoulder pain.

Intervention: Bobath-based techniques to restore scapular/glenohumeral alignment; manual and electrical therapies (ultrasound, cryotherapy, massage) are also recommended.

Prevention & Special Groups

Secondary stroke prevention, vocational rehabilitation, young stroke, and telehealth delivery.

Secondary stroke prevention

Time since first strokeRecurrence rate
3 months7.7%
1 year10.4%
5 years14.8%
12 years39.7%

Systematic review of 37 studies, >1 million patients; rates vary by age, region and stroke subtype, with time since initial stroke the strongest predictor (Lin et al., 2021).

Combining medications, exercise and lifestyle modification can achieve a cumulative relative-risk reduction of 80%. Diet advice: reduce dietary salt, increase potassium intake, reduce saturated fat, increase fruit/vegetables, reduce smoking and alcohol, and control weight.

Exercise target:

At least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week — even short walks or stretching sessions count. Regular physical activity is associated with a 25–30% reduction in stroke risk (evidence largely extrapolated from non-stroke populations, as direct secondary-stroke exercise trials remain limited).

Barriers to exercise participation include lack of interest/motivation, depression, fatigue, joint pain, and doubt about effectiveness — tailor prescription to the individual.

Vocational rehabilitation

Defined as overcoming the barriers an individual faces when accessing, remaining in, or returning to work. Up to 25% of stroke survivors are of working age. A systematic review of 6 clinical trials found return-to-work rates up to 49%. Recommended steps:

  1. Access information about vocational activities early in the rehabilitation pathway
  2. Assess return-to-work potential during daily practice
  3. Discuss with the patient and set vocational rehabilitation goals
  4. Identify the need for job modification
  5. Provide skill-specific training (e.g. use of tools)
  6. Assess skill/ability and match to job demand
  7. Arrange functional capacity evaluation
  8. Provide a work-site visit when necessary

Telehealth rehabilitation

A promising alternative for patients facing mobility, geographic or financial barriers — improving motor function, balance and daily activities, with high patient satisfaction and reduced disability, though methodological variability limits broad conclusions. Delivery tools include video calls, apps, wearables and virtual reality. Priority requirements for acceptance: user safety, user-friendliness, minimal technical maintenance, and minimal home reorganisation/investment. A hybrid model combining in-person and tele-care is now widely adopted.

Discharge & Community

Preparing the patient, home and caregiver for the transition out of hospital-based care.

Home environment evaluation

1a A pre-discharge home visit provides professional advice on environment modification to support ADL and minimise hazards.

Goals

  • Identify potential hazards at home
  • Rehearse the patient's routine and ADL
  • Assess accessibility of community facilities (recreational areas, shops, bus stops)

Criteria for a home visit

  • Potential fall risk from environmental hazards
  • Caring problems due to environmental reasons
  • Need to establish the real environmental situation

Assessment covers indoor items (lighting, entrance threshold, floor obstacles/slip risk, chair/bed/toilet height, mattress firmness, bathroom/kitchen safety, alarm/phone accessibility) and outdoor items (lift availability, stairs, distance to stairs/lift and to recreational areas). Liaise with Medical Social Worker or Housing Authority as needed.

Home exercise prescription

Exercise difficulty should match the patient's ability, with safety the primary concern for unsupervised practice. Home exercise can be practised alone or with caregiver support. Physiotherapists can prescribe home exercise through the Patient Rehab App inside HA Go, allowing the therapist to monitor compliance.

Caregiver education

1a Providing information reduces patient depression scores and enhances satisfaction. 1b Caregiver education has been shown to enhance quality of life and mood, and reduce caregiver stress, at both 3 and 12 months. Education should be tailored and delivered at appropriate time points across rehabilitation phases as needs change.

  • Enhance caregivers' knowledge, skills and competency for subsequent social integration
  • Empower proper, safe handling and caring technique
  • Support transition to a home-based rehabilitation programme with active caregiver participation
  • Provide a home exercise programme
  • Educate on disease process, rehabilitation and physiotherapy management
  • Offer information on community resources such as self-help groups

Outcome Measures

Standardised tools used to track impairment and functional performance and guide ongoing rehabilitation strategy.

Fugl-Meyer Assessment — Upper Extremity (FMA-UE)

33 items, maximum score 66. Scores volitional movement with/without synergies, coordination and reflex action of shoulder, elbow, forearm, wrist and hand on a 3-point scale (0 = unable, 1 = partial, 2 = full). Functional tasks are not included. Reliable and valid for motor impairment.

Timed Up and Go (TUG)

Patient sits with back against the backrest and arms on armrests, stands, walks 3 metres as quickly as possible, turns around a floor marker, walks back and sits again. Time to complete is recorded. High reliability in people with neurological disorders.

Berg Balance Scale

14 items, each scored 0–4 (0 = unable, 4 = independent), maximum 56. Assesses static and dynamic balance across positions of progressively narrowing base of support, from sitting to standing to single-leg stance. Reliable and valid in neurological populations.

Elderly Mobility Scale (EMS)

7 functional activities (transfers, ambulation, balance) plus sit-to-stand and walking speed. Score range 0–20.

ScoreInterpretation
<10Dependent in mobility and basic ADL — long-term/home care package required
10–13Borderline for safe mobility and basic ADL — needs some help
≥14Able to mobilise alone and safely, independent in basic ADL

EMS and its functional-reach component are significantly associated with fall risk.

Modified Functional Ambulatory Category (MFAC)

Developed by HA's Coordinating Committee in Physiotherapy (2007). 7 categories from MFAC I (most dependent) to MFAC VII (independent outdoor walker), rated at the patient's most independent level regardless of assistive device use (sit-to-stand ability excluded). High interrater reliability and concurrent validity.

Modified Barthel Index

10-item tool evaluating baseline activities of daily living. High reliability as a standard assessment in people with stroke.

Modified Rivermead Mobility Index

8 items, each scored 0–5: 0 = no active participation, 1 = assistance of two people, 2 = assistance of one person, 3 = supervision/verbal instruction only, 4 = with aid or appliance, 5 = independent. Good reliability.

Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) Scale

16 items covering indoor and outdoor activities requiring varying balance demands. Patient rates confidence 0–100% for each. Validated for Chinese populations (ABC-C).

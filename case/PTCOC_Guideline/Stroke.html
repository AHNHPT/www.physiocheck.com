How to read the evidence levels

The manual adopts the Evidence-Based Review of Stroke Rehabilitation levels (Teasell et al., 2012), based on the US AHCPR system. These badges appear throughout this page.

Level 1a Strong

Supported by a meta-analysis, or by 2 or more randomised controlled trials of at least “fair” quality.

Level 1b Moderate

Supported by a single RCT of at least “fair” quality.

Level 2 Limited

Supported by at least one controlled trial with a minimum of 10 subjects in each arm.

Level 3 Consensus

In the absence of evidence, agreement by a group of experts. The lowest form of evidence — arguably not evidence at all.

Level 4 Conflicting

Disagreement between the findings of at least 2 RCTs; the majority position prevails unless the conflicting study was of higher quality.

Use with judgement

Evidence grades inform, but do not replace, clinical reasoning. Match the intervention to the patient's condition, mobility level, rehabilitation setting and available resources.

03

Motor impairment & recovery

Chapter 4 · Neuroplasticity, motor learning theory and the two non-negotiable training principles.

Why recovery happens

Motor recovery relates to neuroplasticity — dendritic sprouting, formation of new synapses and alteration of existing synapses. Most rehabilitation protocols are therefore built on motor learning theory: functional gain is greater when training is meaningful, repetitive and intensive. Different combinations of treatment approaches may induce similar functional outcomes — so tailor the design, whatever the approach.

Strong evidence for rehabilitation itself

International guidelines support the effect of rehabilitation in terms of improved functional independence and reduced mortality (National Clinical Guideline for Stroke 2023; Mead et al., 2023). Stroke rehabilitation is a restorative learning process to maximise recovery and reintegrate the survivor into community life.

Task-specific training 1a

Training must be relevant to the patient's functional need and repetitive enough to consolidate motor control. A meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies showed task-specific training produced functional gain accompanied by neural change in the sensorimotor cortex of the affected hemisphere Level 1a. Effectiveness is also shown for lower limb motor performance, sit-to-stand and gait Level 1b–2.

Enriched environment

An environment offering greater opportunity for physical activity and motivation. Animal studies showed training in an enriched environment produced greater motor recovery with enhanced neural plasticity. Design training that is task-specific and delivered in an enriched setting.

Upper limb — prognosis & treatment

Report significant hemiplegic UL impairment~80%
Restore normal function in daily activities<15%
Full UL recovery at 6 months if finger extension present <72 h60%
  • Active finger extension is a strong predictor of short, medium and long term recovery.
  • Shoulder abduction control is another good indicator — patients without proximal arm control have a poor prognosis for regaining hand capacity.
  • CIMT, EMG biofeedback and robotic therapy show potential effect 1a; CIMT effects are robust but applicability is limited (2–3 weeks, 90% of waking hours, 6 h/day training).
  • Low-intensity CIMT — non-paretic limb immobilised <90% of waking hours plus <3 h/day task-oriented training — showed positive effect on arm-hand activities across 23 RCTs / 627 subjects 1a.
  • A Cochrane overview found moderate-quality evidence favouring CIMT, mental practice, mirror therapy, interventions for sensory impairment, virtual reality and a relatively high dose of repetitive task practice 1a.

Mobility & balance

  • Early balance training — at least 20–60 minutes, 3–4 times a week for 6–12 weeks after stroke 1a.
  • Repetitive task training improves sit-to-stand (7 trials, 346 participants) and improves walking speed, functional ambulation and walking distance (Cochrane, 14 trials, 658 participants) 1a.
  • Gait-oriented physical training improves gait speed, endurance and reduces assistance needed — three systematic reviews 1a.
  • Force-plate biofeedback for standing balance — 4 trials, 161 participants 1a; moving platform training also positive (2 trials, 40 subjects).
  • Stair gait training with PNF for 30 minutes improved dynamic balance 1b; walking exercise on stairs enhanced balance 1b.
  • Gait trainer with partial body weight support alongside conventional overground gait training is effective for restoring gait 1b.
  • Promising: hydrotherapy, Bobath approach, Tai Chi — all 1b.

Interventions for motor recovery

Chapter 5 · Robotics · Electrical stimulation · Acupuncture · Virtual reality · Non-invasive brain stimulation. Filter by evidence level.
Filter

Robotics — upper limb Level 1

Devices: ArmeoPower, MIT-Manus, Hand of Hope. A comprehensive review synthesising 16 meta-analyses, 396 RCTs and 19,280 patients (Park et al., 2025) found robot-assisted therapy significantly enhances upper limb motor function and strength — both standalone and alongside conventional therapy at the same intensity — consistently across patient groups, devices and treatment durations.

Robot-assisted gait training Level 1

Exoskeletons (Lokomat, Exowalk, Walkbot, Flexbot-B, EksoGT, HAL, ReWalk) and end-effector robots (G-EO System, Morning Walk, Gait Trainer) showed comparable effects in subacute stroke (13 RCTs, 673 participants). RAGT is not necessarily superior to conventional therapy, but combining RAGT with conventional PT enhances gait function, and early initiation leads to better gait recovery. Evidence on gait biomechanics remains very low quality (13 RCTs, 412 participants).

Robotic assisted gait training — balance Level 3 Level 1

RAGT provides controlled, complex multi-sensory stimulation considered essential for balance recovery L3 and enables earlier upright positioning with graded weight-bearing. Exoskeleton RAT gave significantly greater Berg Balance Scale gains than conventional therapy in acute/subacute stroke (13 RCTs, 517 participants) but not in chronic stroke L1; a later review of 18 RCTs found no BBS or TUG advantage L1.

Bottom line: robotic therapy yields greatest benefit when integrated with conventional rehabilitation, particularly in the acute and subacute stages.

Electrical stimulation — upper limb Level 1

Two broad types: FES (stimulating several muscle groups to replace/assist voluntary contraction during a functional task) and cyclical stimulation (repeated maximal contraction to strengthen). A review of 25 studies showed FES-based rehabilitation significantly improved UL function on FMA and ARAT; both open- and closed-loop systems improved outcomes, with EMG-controlled FES giving the largest effect (FMA MD up to 14.14; ARAT MD 11.9; p<0.001).

Electrical stimulation — lower limb & gait Level 1

Six systematic reviews (57 studies) could reach no concrete recommendation due to variability. More recently: FES alone did not significantly enhance gait speed but did improve ankle dorsiflexion, balance and functional mobility (14 studies, 1,115 patients); FES with supervised exercise gave greater gait-speed gains. NMES (29 studies, 1,711 survivors) improved gait speed, cadence, step length, dorsiflexion and functional mobility, and may be more effective in chronic phases with shorter sessions (~20–30 min) — longer sessions may raise neural excitability thresholds and cause adaptation.

Cyclical electrical stimulation Level 1

A review of 16 studies concluded it can increase muscle strength and improve activity performance with carry-over, but the trials did not show superiority over other strengthening interventions, and optimal stimulation and dosage remain unclear. Standardised protocols are still needed. A recent review identified target muscles, stroke severity, post-stroke timing, dose, duration and mode as critical determinants of NMES effectiveness L3.

Acupuncture — motor function Level 2

Additional acupuncture on top of conventional treatment gave extra motor recovery benefit in sub-acute stroke L2. Electroacupuncture produced similar motor gains but enhanced corticospinal tract integrity, promoting plasticity and reducing neural degeneration L2. Acupuncture combined with conventional rehabilitation reduced post-stroke spasticity (88 studies, 6,431 participants), with benefit increasing with frequency and number of sessions L1. In HA, an operational guideline for physiotherapists on acupuncture practice has been in place since 2009 and is reviewed every three years.

Acupuncture — hemiplegic shoulder pain Level 1

Acupuncture combined with exercise was effective in reducing hemiplegic shoulder pain (7 RCTs, 502 participants). A meta-analysis of 40 trials / 2,554 patients found acupuncture plus rehabilitation outperformed rehabilitation alone for pain, motor function, daily activities and shoulder ROM, with no significant adverse effects — though heterogeneity means more rigorous research is needed.

Virtual reality Level 1

Immersive VR (head-mounted displays, large projections) creates a stronger sense of presence; non-immersive VR (standard screens) is more accessible and adaptable for clinical use. Both can produce cortical activation and promote motor recovery.

  • Upper limb: VR training is more effective than conventional therapy; haptic gloves + VR added to conventional therapy give significantly greater UL functional gains.
  • Balance & gait: VR gives superior balance recovery vs conventional therapy, is more effective in the chronic than the acute phase, and significantly enhances walking speed and cadence.

rTMS — repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

Mechanism — based on the interhemispheric inhibition model: impairment arises not only from lesioned-hemisphere damage but also from excessive inhibition by the contralesional hemisphere. High-frequency rTMS (>1 Hz) to the lesioned hemisphere boosts excitability; low-frequency rTMS (≤1 Hz) suppresses the overactive contralesional hemisphere.

Motor recovery — clinical results are inconsistent, likely from small samples and methodological differences. rTMS may be particularly effective in patients with an MEP response, subcortical lesions, mild paresis and preserved baseline function.

tDCS — transcranial direct current stimulation

Mechanism — a current stimulator with saline-soaked scalp electrodes delivers a steady low-intensity direct current (typically 1–2 mA). The active electrode sits over the target cortex, the reference over a neutral area such as the supraorbital region. Anodal stimulation generally increases excitability; cathodal decreases it.

Motor recovery — no robust concordance between studies, mainly due to heterogeneity of clinical data, tDCS approaches, combined interventions and outcome measures. tDCS may promote adaptive plasticity with positive pre- and post-motor rehabilitation effects, and anodal tDCS may improve paretic upper limb function in chronic stroke.

Sensory & perceptual impairment

Chapter 6 · Highly prevalent, frequently under-assessed, and a significant predictor of balance dysfunction.

How common are sensory deficits?

Loss of touch sensationup to 94%
Loss of proprioception89%
Somatic sensation deficits reportedup to 85%
Loss of pinprick sensationup to 71%
Sensory deficit in the ipsilesional upper limb64%
Don't assume the "good" side is normal. Bilateral proprioceptive deficits — including at the ankle — do occur and are significant predictors of balance dysfunction.

What to do about it 1a evidence gap

High-quality trials are lacking and meta-analysis could not be performed Level 1a. Individual studies and recent reviews nonetheless show positive, low-to-moderate quality effects:

  • Repetitive thermal stimulation (alternating heating/cooling of the hand) added to standard therapy improved sensation over six weeks and gave greater motor recovery.
  • Systematic sensory retraining 3×/week for 6 weeks improved location of touch, elbow proprioception, two-point discrimination and stereognosis.
  • Perceptual-learning sensory discrimination — texture discrimination, limb position sense, tactile object recognition.
  • Intensive active sensory–motor programmes with varied textures and object sizes improve tactile and proprioceptive sensibility.
  • Mirror therapy reduces CRPS pain and enhances motor recovery.
  • Tactile stimulation and TENS combined with OT/PT may enhance sensory–motor recovery.
  • Multisensory integration (MSI) may outperform single-modality stimulation, though study quality is insufficient to conclude effectiveness. A low-risk intervention with positive outcomes.
Sensory and motor systems are interdependent but may recover independently — assess and target somatosensory function explicitly rather than assuming it follows motor gains.

Perceptual impairment & spatial neglect

Estimated prevalence of spatial neglect after unilateral stroke is approximately 30%, higher after right brain damage. Ecological assessments are preferred for detection.

  • Transcutaneous electrical stimulation over the dorsal sternocleidomastoid contralateral to the damaged hemisphere reduced postural instability in patients with neglect.
  • Electrical stimulation to the forearm of the neglected side whenever the contralateral arm moved improved perception.
  • Mirror therapy — limited evidence for visuospatial neglect; task-based and magnified mirror therapy protocols may enhance motor and functional recovery.
  • MSI combining visual, auditory, tactile or proprioceptive stimulation may improve neglect.
  • Prism adaptation treatment (PAT) — more sessions predict greater improvement in neglect and rehabilitation outcomes, and may reduce in-hospital falls.
A recent Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute interventions for perceptual disorders after stroke. Continue neurorehabilitation according to clinical guidelines while the evidence base matures.

Technology in sensory rehabilitation

VR and robot-assisted rehabilitation are increasingly integrated into practice, with evidence supporting improvement in upper limb motor function and proprioception — though not necessarily superior to conventional therapies.

Robot-assisted therapy combining motor training with somatosensory and cognitive tasks has shown non-inferior motor recovery compared with dose-matched conventional neurocognitive therapy.

Barriers to adoption: cost, usability and space constraints — weigh these against expected gain before requesting equipment.

Remember the underlying principle: repeated motor practice induces neuroplasticity in both sensory and motor networks, and the plasticity induced is highly specific to the activity practised and the practice dosage.

Psychological · Communication · Cognitive

Chapters 7–9 · Three domains where the physiotherapist's role is to observe, adapt and refer.

Recognising the problem

Mood disturbance is common after stroke and diagnosis is difficult, particularly in the presence of speech disturbance.

Depression: crying, feeling miserable or hopeless, lack of motivation, loss of appetite, reduced social activities.
Anxiety: an unpleasant, uncontrollable effect of fear or apprehension, often with breathlessness, palpitations and trembling — may be provoked by fear of falling during transfer or fear of meeting people.

CBT for post-stroke patients (10 RCTs; 432 treated vs 385 controls) significantly improved depression severity and quality of life, but showed no significant difference in cognitive function.

The physiotherapist's role

Aim — observe the presence of psychological disturbance, support patients and caregivers, and refer to other disciplines for further intervention.

  • General psychological support to patient and caregivers — provide information about the illness; provide support and encouragement
  • Refer as necessary — to doctor for pharmacological therapy, to clinical psychologist, to support groups

Frequent observation and communication to identify abnormal behaviour or signs of anxiety, emotionalism and depression are essential for holistic rehabilitation. Poor motivation to exercise may be the presenting sign. Give patients information, advice and the opportunity to talk about the impact of the illness on their lives.

What you may encounter

Stroke can cause dysarthria, apraxia and aphasia as a result of motor or cognitive impairment.

Aim — observe the presence of communication impairment and adopt the necessary approach to facilitate effective communication.

Communication strategy is not an optional courtesy — it is what makes the rest of your treatment session possible.

Recommended communication strategies

  • Eliminate background noise
  • Ensure the patient's attention before communicating
  • Encourage all modes of communication — speech, writing, drawing, yes/no responses
  • Permit a reasonable amount of time to respond
  • Accept all communication attempts
  • Use simple adult communication
  • Simplify sentence structure and reduce speech rate
  • Emphasise key words
  • Augment speech with gesture and visual aids where possible
  • Repeat statements when necessary

Impact on rehabilitation

Stroke may cause neglect, inattention, apraxia or dyspraxia, and difficulties with attention, memory and executive functioning. Cognitive impairment adversely affects the ability to participate in therapy and ADL, and can be confusing and distressing for patients and caregivers.

Several types of cognitive impairment may occur at the same time. An apparent lack of motivation in self-care could be due to a problem in initiating or planning actions, visuospatial disturbance, or both.

Emerging: scalp stimulation for post-stroke cognitive impairment and dementia — different cognitive domains have different optimal prescriptions, and activating affected key brain regions while inhibiting the unaffected area remains the most effective approach. Electro-acupuncture has also been shown to help post-stroke cognitive dysfunction.

Recommended actions

Aim — screen for cognitive impairment, then modify communication approaches, rehabilitation plans and goals.

  • Improve the patient's motivation for rehabilitation
  • Minimise attentional demands — shorten training intervals, build in rest breaks, use a less distracting environment
  • Use internal or external strategies via different input routes (verbal, visual, tactile)
  • Use compensatory techniques, e.g. checklists, to improve daily-activity performance
  • Discuss with the patient, family, staff and others involved
Screen as soon as practicable, explain the impact to patients, caregivers and staff, direct to neuropsychological expertise when appropriate, and address safety risks from persisting cognitive impairment during discharge planning.

Ambulatory aids & wheelchair prescription

Chapter 10 · Walking aids enhance gait pattern and stability — but an inappropriate prescription costs energy and attention.

Evidence you should know

  • Quad canes may be more effective than standard canes at decreasing postural sway in moderate hemiparesis during stance.
  • Cane length at the greater trochanter may give more normal walking of the affected limb than wrist-crease fitting.
  • A cane improves walking speed only while it is being used — benefits are not carried over to walking without it.
  • One-arm motorised gait devices may outperform parallel bars and hemi-walkers in chronic hemiplegic survivors.
  • Walking aids do not worsen the asymmetrical weight-bearing pattern characteristic of hemiparesis.
  • Incorrect use of walking frames is common and is associated with reduced stability — guidance must address environmental constraints.
  • Assistive walking devices improve functional mobility in non-ambulant rehabilitation patients, and patients are generally positive about using them.
  • Exercise-based interventions may reduce the rate of falls after stroke; evidence for other interventions is limited.

Walking aid prescription

Preparation

  • Assess sensorimotor function for potential walking ability
  • Deliver training with the proper aid and explore different walking environments
  • Caregiver training if the patient walks with assistance
  • Fall prevention education
  • Information about walking aid maintenance

Measurement

  • Height measured to wrist crease or greater trochanter — evidence suggests GT fitting may be preferable
  • Practise walking with the aid for further height adjustment
Common aids: walking stick, quadripod, walking frame, rollator — plus newer one-arm motorised gait devices. Choice depends on the patient's ability and the walking environment.

Wheelchair prescription

Indicated when the patient cannot walk due to motor impairment, cognitive impairment or decline in physical function. Types: standard, transit, one-arm drive for self-propulsion, and electric when necessary.

  • Assess needs and indoor and outdoor accessibility of the living environment
  • Caregiver training on manipulation over level ground, slope, kerb
  • Self-propelling users should have no major cognitive deficit or spatial orientation problem
  • Educate proper transfer technique
  • Pressure awareness and relieving techniques
  • Maintenance instructions · regular review of fit and function

Measurements: seat width, seat depth, seat height, armrest height, seat-back height. Selection: wheel type (solid/air), footrest (fixed/detachable), armrest (full-length or desk length; fixed or removable/flip-back) and accessories, depending on need.

The biomechanical consequences of assisted gait are highly variable and depend on individual characteristics, device configuration and environmental conditions. Appropriate selection, height adjustment and weight-bearing support enhance functional compensation, improve gait and reduce joint loads — while inappropriate prescriptions increase energy expenditure and cognitive demand.

Complications management

Chapter 11 · Patients at risk: low consciousness, multiple comorbidities, severe mobility impairment. Complications reduce survival, hinder rehabilitation potential and lengthen stay.

Fall prevention

Report a fall in the first week (acute stage)~7%
Fall incidence across stroke stages25–73%

More than half of patients report a fall by 6 months post-stroke. Beyond injury, a fall history causes loss of confidence and fear of a second fall, which eventually hinders mobility.

  • Prevention: identify fall risk factors by balance assessments and motion-related measures
  • Intervention: exercises such as balance training and dual-task exercises
Falls most commonly occur during sit-to-stand transfer or the reverse. The first fall is significantly associated with visuospatial neglect; repeated falls with age >65. Exercise appears to reduce the rate of falls but not the number of people falling; a local study found dual-task training more effective for dual-task mobility and for reducing falls and fall-related injuries in ambulatory chronic stroke. Currently no programme is proven effective at reducing falls post-stroke.

Chest complication

Common in patients with low conscious level and poor mobility. Coughing is a key defence mechanism against aspiration and pneumonia; a weakened cough reflex and impaired swallowing are linked to aspiration pneumonia. A strong cough protects against aspiration pneumonia and can be used to predict pneumonia risk.

  • Prevention: chest physiotherapy to improve or maintain cough effort
  • Intervention: combination of chest mobilisation and breathing exercises
Chest mobilisation plus breathing exercises improved respiratory muscle mobility and endurance, stabilised the trunk, and enhanced balance and weight transfer in chronic stroke patients.

Pressure sore

Develops in patients who are comatose, severely paralysed, obese, incontinent or spastic. Most common sites: buttock, heels. SIGN states pressure sores should not develop due to immobility after stroke if there are adequate resources and expertise.

  • Prevention: identify high-risk patients · evaluate skin at regular intervals · appropriate bed and chair equipment · change positions at regular intervals · ensure patient mobility
  • Intervention: early mobilisation · positioning · neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES)

Limb oedema & DVT

Common because of decreased mobility. Early mobilisation is effective for prevention (SIGN 2010).

  • Prevention: assess for signs of DVT · early mobilisation, both active and passive · educate caregivers on limb mobilisation
  • Intervention: intermittent pneumatic compression therapy · compression stockings to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome in patients with proximal DVT after anticoagulation therapy

Spasticity & contracture

Spasticity = velocity-dependent hyperexcitability of muscle to stretch, presenting as exaggerated tendon reflex, increased resistance to passive movement and hypertonia. It is an independent risk factor for post-stroke pain — stretching a spastic contracted muscle can disrupt muscle fibres and release substances exciting muscle nociceptors.

  • Prevention: proper positioning · limb mobilisation · stretching programme · caregiver education · passive standing by tilt-table or standing wheelchair · TENS, ultrasound and paraffin therapy
  • Intervention: as above, with precaution on the ROM limit of the particular joint
Splinting is not recommended (SIGN 2010) — existing RCTs found no additional benefit. TENS, ultrasound and paraffin therapy bring broadly equal benefit in managing post-stroke spasticity.

Hemiplegic shoulder

Shoulder pain affects 24% of stroke patients up to 16 months post-stroke. Causes: spasticity, subluxation, poor positioning, and underlying disorders such as adhesive capsulitis.

Early detection and prompt intervention prevent loss of shoulder range and the development of shoulder–hand syndrome.
  • Positioning stretch — a 30-minute programme in maximal comfortable shoulder external rotation and 90° flexion significantly reduced contracture development 1b
  • Electrical stimulation to supraspinatus and deltoid restores humeral head alignment to the glenoid fossa 1a
  • Kinesio taping may realign trunk, scapula and shoulder — but clinical trials are lacking 3
  • Active approaches: tone normalisation, strengthening, manual activation by Bobath or PNF
  • Manual and electrical therapies — ultrasound, cryotherapy, massage
Current evidence does not support strapping or a shoulder sling for the management of hemiplegic shoulder pain (SIGN 2010).

Positioning the hemiplegic patient

Change position every 2 to 3 hours. Proper positioning reduces shoulder pain, modulates muscle tone, provides sensory information and increases spatial awareness. Scapular depression and downward rotation are common; the arm is typically adducted and internally rotated, elbow flexed, forearm pronated, wrist and fingers flexed — so retrain the agonists: scapular elevation and retraction, shoulder abduction and external rotation, elbow extension, forearm supination, wrist–finger extension.

Avoid horizontal adduction of the hemiplegic shoulder when side-lying on the unaffected side — it can traction the suprascapular structures and cause shoulder pain.

Prevention of secondary stroke

Chapter 12 · Recurrence risk climbs steeply with time — and combined approaches cut it dramatically.

Recurrence risk over time

Systematic review of 37 studies, over 1 million patients. Recurrence varied by age, region and stroke subtype — with time since the initial stroke the strongest predictor.

At 3 months7.7%
At 1 year10.4%
At 5 years14.8%
At 12 years39.7%
Combining medication + exercise + lifestyle modification results in a cumulative relative risk reduction of 80%. Patients should be encouraged to take responsibility for their own health and supported to identify and change lifestyle risk factors.

Exercise & diet

Physical activity reduces stroke risk by 25–30%. Target at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise weekly — even short walks or stretching sessions count. Higher walking pace and distance are associated with lower risk of first stroke.

Exercise should focus on task-oriented models combining progressive aerobic training with motor learning, improving both cardiovascular fitness and motor performance. Treadmill training and hydrotherapy improved cardiovascular performance in stroke patients. Declining aerobic fitness further increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and recurrent stroke.

Dietary measures: reduce dietary salt · increase potassium intake · reduce saturated fat · increase fruit and vegetable consumption · reduce smoking and alcohol · weight control.

Barriers to exercise participation: lack of interest and motivation, depression, fatigue, joint pain, and doubt about the effectiveness and feasibility of exercise. Prescriptions must be tailored to the individual. Although evidence on exercise for secondary stroke prevention is limited, AHA/ASA still encourages continuous participation in exercise programmes after stroke.

Vocational rehab · Young stroke · Telehealth

Chapters 13–14 · Up to 25% of stroke survivors are of working age, and young stroke is rising fast in Hong Kong.

Ch 13 · Vocational rehabilitation

Defined as “the process of overcoming the barriers an individual faces when accessing, remaining in or returning to work”. Work — paid or other meaningful activity — meets financial and psychological needs; patients may lose self-esteem if they cannot return to work even when basic ADLs are intact.

Six clinical trials identified; conclusions limited by trial quality, but return-to-work rates reached up to 49%.

  1. Access information about vocational activities early in the rehabilitation pathway
  2. Assess return-to-work potential during daily practice
  3. Discuss and set vocational rehabilitation goals with the patient
  4. Identify the need for job modification
  5. Provide skill-specific training, e.g. use of tools
  6. Match the patient's skills and abilities to job demand
  7. Arrange functional capacity evaluation
  8. Provide a work-site visit when necessary

Ch 14 · Young stroke

Annual incidence in adults under 50 ranges from 3 to 44.3 per 100,000. About 20% of all stroke patients are under 65 and 5% under 45. In Hong Kong, young stroke incidence rose 30% from 2001 to 2021 (1,531 → 2,028 cases admitted to public hospitals).

  • Rehabilitation is broadly the same as for older patients, but rehabilitation potential and demand are greater; FIM improvement is negatively related to age.
  • Young patients gain greater relative benefit from stroke unit care.
  • Suggested triage: patients <55 with severe stroke (FIM <40), and patients <75 with moderate severity (FIM 40–60), to intensive inpatient rehabilitation for 4–6 weeks.
  • Unique needs from pre-morbid vocation, family role, economic burden and social role — functional capacity evaluation and advanced instrumental balance/gait retraining.
  • ~27% of young stroke patients have post-stroke depression; they are interested in connecting with others sharing similar experiences — encourage community support organisations.

Community rehab & telehealth

Early supported discharge (ESD) with home or community rehabilitation may reduce length of stay and long-term dependency, especially for mild and moderate stroke — and total cost is significantly lower than usual care. There is strong (Level 1a) evidence that early-discharged young stroke patients can be successfully rehabilitated by an interdisciplinary team in the community.

Key benefits of community rehab: improves daily living skills and neurological recovery · reduces deterioration in mobility and independence · enhances quality of life and social participation · supports caregivers through education and respite.

Telerehabilitation significantly improves motor function, balance and daily activities with high patient satisfaction and reduced disability; a review of 13 RCTs found positive impact across a range of outcomes, though methodological variability limits generalisation.

For a teletherapy system to be accepted it must assure user safety, be user-friendly, avoid frequent technical maintenance, and require minimal home reorganisation and investment. Hybrid in-person + tele-care is now widely adopted.

FMA-UE
Fugl-Meyer Assessment — Upper Extremity
33 items · max score 66
Tap to flip ›
Motor recovery of post-stroke hemiplegia, all ages. Assesses volitional movement with and without synergies, coordination and reflex action of shoulder, elbow, forearm, wrist and hand. Functional tasks are not included. Graded 0 = unable, 1 = partial, 2 = full. The evaluator may assist to stabilise the arm when testing wrist and hand. Equipment: reflex hammer, scrap of paper, tennis ball, small can, pencil, stopwatch. Reliable and valid for motor impairment.
TUG

Mobility · timed, 3 metres
Tap to flip ›
Mobility performance. Sit on a chair with back against the backrest and arms on the armrests, stand up, walk 3 metres as quickly as possible, turn around a floor marker, walk back and sit down again with the back against the backrest. Time to complete is recorded. High reliability in people with neurological disorders. Equipment: chair with back and armrests, stopwatch, floor marker.
BBS
Berg Balance Scale
14 items · 0–4 each · max 56
Tap to flip ›
Static and dynamic balance. Reliable and valid for neurological patients. Each item scored 0 (unable) to 4 (independent); items progressively narrow the base of support from sitting, to standing, to single-leg stance. Also used in elderly and other groups such as Parkinson's disease.
EMS

Mobility status of frail elderly. Covers transfers, ambulatory level and balance; speed of sit-to-stand and walking speed are analysed.

Case 1 · Acute Stroke Unit · Day 1
Madam L, 78, is admitted with a large right MCA infarct. She is drowsy but rousable, with dense left hemiplegia, left neglect and a weak, ineffective cough. Blood pressure is labile and she remains nil-by-mouth pending a swallowing assessment. The medical team has not yet declared her neurologically stable. You are the physiotherapist covering the acute stroke unit.

According to the manual, what should drive your physiotherapy plan today?

✕ IncorrectThe manual is explicit: following the HA Acute Ischaemic Stroke Care Guideline, physiotherapists should assess stroke patients within one working day of admission and provide supportive treatment in an Acute Stroke Unit. Instability changes what you do, not whether you assess.
✓ CorrectDuring the initial phase, stabilisation of the medical and neurological condition is the prime objective, so treatment emphasises prevention and management of secondary complications resulting from stroke, such as limb contracture and chest complications. Only after stabilisation do you move to prioritising problems across the five domains and setting up a full treatment plan. With a weak cough, chest physiotherapy to improve or maintain cough effort is a legitimate acute priority — a strong cough protects against aspiration pneumonia and predicts pneumonia risk.
✕ IncorrectCIMT effects are robust Level 1a, but applicability is limited: it targets patients with limited arm impairment who can tolerate prolonged constraint (2–3 weeks, 90% of waking hours) and 6 hours of training per day. A drowsy patient with dense hemiplegia and neglect is not a CIMT candidate, and constraining the only functioning limb in a patient with neglect is unsafe.
✕ IncorrectTwo separate recommendations are breached here. Splinting is not recommended — existing RCTs found no additional benefit (SIGN 2010). And current evidence does not support strapping or a shoulder sling for managing hemiplegic shoulder pain. Use proper positioning, limb mobilisation and a stretching programme instead.
Case 2 · Extended care ward · Day 4
Mr C, 62, had a left subcortical infarct four days ago. On assessment he has active finger extension of the right hand (first observed at 48 hours post-onset) and minimal but present shoulder abduction. He is alert, follows instruction, and asks you directly: “Will my hand come back?” A colleague suggests booking him for daily robotic upper-limb therapy instead of his conventional sessions.

Which response best reflects the evidence in the manual?

✕ IncorrectIt is true that around 80% of stroke patients report significant hemiplegic upper limb impairment and fewer than 15% restore normal function in daily activities. But those are population figures — this patient carries two of the strongest positive predictors. Applying the population average to a favourable individual is a reasoning error, and premature compensation forfeits recoverable function.
✕ IncorrectThe manual states the opposite: patients with minimal shoulder abduction and upper motor control of the paretic limb on admission had a reasonably good chance of regaining some hand capacity, whereas those without proximal arm control had a poor prognosis for hand capacity. Control of shoulder abduction is described as another good indicator of upper extremity recovery.
✓ CorrectActive finger extension is a strong predictor of short, medium and long-term recovery, and 60% of patients with finger extension within 72 hours regained full upper limb function on the Action Research Arm Test at 6 months. Shoulder abduction control is a further good indicator. Treatment should be task-specific, repetitive and delivered in an enriched environment 1a. Robot-assisted therapy enhances UL motor function and strength both standalone and alongside conventional therapy at the same intensity (16 meta-analyses, 396 RCTs, 19,280 patients) — the manual's conclusion is that the greatest benefit comes from integration with conventional rehabilitation, especially in acute and subacute stages.
✕ IncorrectSuperiority over conventional therapy is not what the evidence shows. For gait, exoskeletons and end-effector robots are comparable to conventional therapy, and no significant difference was observed between RAGT and conventional therapy; for balance, findings are mixed. The consistent message is that robotic therapy adds value when combined with conventional rehabilitation — not that it substitutes for it.
Case 3 · Rehabilitation ward · Week 3
Madam W, 70, is three weeks post right hemispheric stroke. Her left arm remains flaccid with two-finger-breadth glenohumeral subluxation. Over the past week she reports new left shoulder pain on movement; passive external rotation is limited and painful. She sleeps mostly on her right (unaffected) side, and the ward has been positioning the left arm across her chest. There are no signs of a neuropathic component and no history of trauma or fall.

Which management package is most consistent with the manual?

✓ CorrectEvery element is supported. Horizontal adduction of the hemiplegic shoulder must be avoided when side-lying on the unaffected side — it tractions the suprascapular structures and causes shoulder pain, which is exactly what the ward's current positioning is producing. A 30-minute positioning programme in maximal comfortable external rotation and 90° flexion significantly reduced contracture development Level 1b. Electrical stimulation of supraspinatus and deltoid restores humeral head alignment to the glenoid fossa Level 1a. Caregiver education on positioning and handling is explicitly recommended.
✕ IncorrectThe manual states plainly that current evidence does not support the use of strapping and shoulder sling for the management of hemiplegic shoulder pain (SIGN 2010). Kinesio taping is mentioned as a possible approach for realigning trunk, scapula and shoulder because of its elastic property, but it is Level 3 — there is a lack of clinical trials.
✕ IncorrectIn the SIGN-based algorithm, X-ray and orthopaedic opinion are considered when possible injury is suspected, and referral for further investigation such as MRI comes only after persistent symptoms despite treatment. With no trauma history, the correct next step is physiotherapy, analgesia and monitoring — escalating only if symptoms persist.
✕ IncorrectProlonged immobilisation of the hemiplegic shoulder causes limb contracture and pain — around half of stroke patients suffer significant reduction in shoulder external rotation and elevation. Early detection and prompt intervention are needed to prevent loss of range and the development of shoulder–hand syndrome. Rest is not a neutral option here.
Case 4 · Physiotherapy Outpatient Department
Mr F, 68, is 14 months post right hemispheric stroke and walks indoors and outdoors with a single-point stick. He has residual left visuospatial neglect. He reports two falls in the past three months, both while rising from a chair, and now avoids going out because he is afraid of falling again. His wife asks you to “give him something that will stop the falls.”

What is the most appropriate response, based on the manual?

✕ IncorrectThe manual cites evidence that use of a cane improves walking speed only when participants walk with the cane — benefits are not maintained when walking without it. There is also no basis for promising that a walking aid alone will prevent falls; incorrect use of walking aids is common and is associated with reduced stability.
✕ IncorrectWheelchair prescription is recommended when a patient cannot walk due to motor impairment, cognitive impairment or decline in physical function — not as a blanket response to fall history in a functioning community walker. Withdrawing ambulation would reinforce the loss of confidence and fear of a second fall, which the manual identifies as precisely what hinders the mobility of stroke survivors.
✕ IncorrectOverstated. Exercises appear to reduce the rate of falls but not the number of people falling post-stroke (Cochrane 2019), and the manual notes that currently no programme is effective in reducing falls in people post-stroke. Exercise remains the best available intervention — but it should be offered honestly, not as a guarantee.
✓ CorrectThis matches the manual's fall prevention recommendations exactly: preventive measure — identify fall risk factors by balance assessments and motion-related measures; intervention — exercises such as balance training and dual-task exercises. A meta-analysis found balance and motion-related measures to be the most common and significant fall risk factors post-stroke. His profile fits the literature closely: falls occur most commonly during sit-to-stand transfer or the reverse, and the first fall after stroke is significantly associated with visuospatial neglect. A local study found dual-task training more effective for improving dual-task mobility and reducing falls and fall-related injuries in ambulatory chronic stroke.
Case 5 · Extended care · Discharge planning meeting
Madam T, 80, is eight weeks post stroke and has plateaued. Her Elderly Mobility Scale score is 9 and she is MFAC III. She lives with her daughter, who works full time, in a flat reached by two flights of stairs with no lift. The daughter has not yet had any handling training. The team asks for your recommendation.

Which recommendation is best supported by the manual?

✕ IncorrectThe cut-offs are misread. On the EMS, a score between 10 and 13 indicates borderline safe mobility and basic ADL requiring some help. A score lower than 10 — as here — indicates the subject is dependent in mobility manoeuvres and basic ADL, and a long-term care or home care package is required.
✓ CorrectEMS <10 = dependent in mobility manoeuvres and basic ADL; long-term care or home care package required. The extended-care discharge criteria require that a safe place of residence is identified according to physical, environmental and psychosocial status, that patients and caregivers have received training on caring skills, and that home modification and prescribed aids are suggested or arranged. Pre-discharge home environment evaluation is recommended Level 1a — particularly relevant here with two flights of stairs and no lift — and liaison with the Medical Social Worker or agencies such as the Housing Authority is recommended after the evaluation. Caregiver education reduced patients' depression scores and improved satisfaction 1a, and enhanced caregiver quality of life and mood while reducing stress at 3 and 12 months 1b.
✕ IncorrectThe MFAC scale runs the other way: MFAC I indicates the most dependent subjects and MFAC VII the independent outdoor walker. MFAC III is at the dependent end of the range. Note also that subjects are rated at their most independent level regardless of assistive device use, and the ability to rise from sitting to standing is not included in the rating.
✕ IncorrectPlateau appears as a discharge criterion for domiciliary physiotherapy and physiotherapy outpatient service, but the criteria are a set of conditions to be considered together — including caregiver training, home exercise prescription, prescribed aids and home modifications to address safety risks. In acute/extended care the criteria also require a safe place of residence and arranged continuing services. Stopping at “plateau” alone leaves an untrained caregiver, an unassessed home and no support package.

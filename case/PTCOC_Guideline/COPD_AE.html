AECOPD — an event characterised by dyspnoea and/or cough and sputum that worsen over <14 days. Associated with accelerated lung-function decline, poorer quality of life and increased mortality.
Overview

The four jobs of physiotherapy in AECOPD

Every intervention in this guideline is in service of one of these goals. Patient population: adults admitted with an AECOPD (chronic bronchitis, emphysema, chronic airflow limitation). Critically ill patients requiring invasive/non-invasive ventilatory support are outside its scope.

Reduce dyspnoea

Breathing retraining, positioning and adjuncts to ease the sensation of breathlessness.

Improve exercise capacity

Preserve muscle strength and physical activity through and beyond the exacerbation.

Improve mucus clearance

Match the airway-clearance technique to sputum load, expiratory force and collapse risk.

Build self-efficacy

Knowledge, self-management skills and confidence to reduce future admissions.

How to read the evidence badges

Every recommendation card carries two independent ratings: a Level (quality of the underlying studies) and a Grade (strength of the recommendation built on that evidence — SIGN 2011). A grade reflects evidence strength, not clinical importance — some of the best-graded evidence here supports not doing something.

Assess, analyse, then plan

Every episode of care follows the same three steps before a single technique is chosen.

Inspection
Palpation

History taking
  • History of the current admission
  • Past history: asthma, allergy, sinusitis/polyps, childhood respiratory infection
  • Prior exacerbations or hospitalisations — patients may not recognise past episodes as "exacerbations"
  • Comorbidities that also restrict activity (cardiac disease, malignancy, osteoporosis, musculoskeletal disorders)
  • Exposure to risk factors — smoking, occupational/environmental exposure
  • Impact on life: activity limits, missed work, mood, family routines, social support
Analysis — what you're pulling together
  • Medical referral data, labs, CXR, pulmonary function tests
  • Whether medical treatment has already been optimised
  • Dyspnoea, reduced exercise performance and physical activity
  • Peripheral & respiratory muscle tests, 6-minute walk test
  • Airway clearance: impaired clearance, mucus quality/quantity, cough efficacy
Treatment plan — the 4 goals in practice
  • Reduce or eliminate functional impairment
  • Improve participation in activity
  • Improve quality of life
  • Reduce dyspnoea · improve exercise capacity · improve mucus clearance · improve self-management

The COPD Assessment Test (CAT)

An 8-item, self-scored questionnaire (0–5 per item, 0–40 total) that quantifies the impact of COPD on daily life. GOLD-recommended for assessing and monitoring COPD (Jones et al 2009).

0–10Mild impact
11–20Moderate impact
21–30Severe impact
31–40Very severe
A change of ≥2 points is clinically significant · roughly a 5-point gap is typically seen between stable COPD and an active exacerbation.
02 · Chapter 2

Managing dyspnoea

"Breathing exercises" is an umbrella term — the six approaches below range from strongly recommended to actively cautioned. Match the technique to the patient in front of you.

A
LEVEL 1++
Recommend

Inspiratory muscle training

Best-evidenced adjunct in the whole dyspnoea chapter. For patients with dyspnoea who can't manage standard exercise training and show reduced inspiratory muscle strength. Controlled/threshold load ≥30% of maximal inspiratory pressure.

Geddes 2005 · Gosselink 2011 · Beaumont 2018
C
LEVEL 2−
Consider

Pursed-lips breathing

Reduces breathing frequency and dyspnoea, improves SpO₂, exercise endurance and tidal volume. Test the response in each individual; use during dyspnoea-provoking activity.

Bianchi 2004 · Cabral 2015
B
LEVEL 1−
Not recommended

Diaphragmatic breathing

Not recommended for moderate–severe COPD — can increase work of breathing and provoke asynchronous / paradoxical movement. Modify or stop if abdominal paradox, worsening dyspnoea or fatigue appear.

Gosselink 1995 · Cahalin 2002
B
LEVEL 1−
Consider

Relaxation exercises

Short-term benefit for breathing frequency, dyspnoea and anxiety. Worth considering in anxious, breathless patients.

Brenes 2003
C
LEVEL 2−
Recommend

Body positioning — forward lean

Greater lung volumes, less accessory-muscle EMG activity, improved diaphragm function and reduced dyspnoea. Also effective during rollator-assisted walking.

Gosselink 2003 · Kim 2012
B
LEVEL 1−
Consider

AcuTENS / acupressure

TENS at acupoint EX-B1 or acupressure over several points has improved FEV₁, dyspnoea and CAT score in small trials. A reasonable additional adjunct.

Lau & Jones 2008 · Maa 1997
03 · Chapter 3

Airway clearance & mucus management

Most trials behind this chapter are small and of mixed quality — read every "recommend" with appropriate caution, and pick the technique that matches the mechanical problem (weak cough vs. localized retention vs. airway collapse).

C
LEVEL 2−
Consider

Postural drainage

Lacks evidence as a standalone technique, but useful alongside forced expiratory manoeuvres or PEP when those are insufficient, or with localized mucus retention.

Ides 2011 · Bott 2009
B
LEVEL 2++
Not recommended

Chest percussion & vibration

No added benefit over postural drainage and coughing; sputum yield not statistically significant. Probably not effective as a routine technique in AECOPD.

Savci 2000 · Cross 2012
C
LEVEL 2−
Selected patients

Manual chest / abdominal compression

Increases peak cough flow in muscle weakness — but decreases it in typical COPD patients. Reserve for patients with genuine expiratory muscle weakness.

Sivasothy 2001
C
LEVEL 2−
Recommend

Forced expiratory technique / ACBT

Treat patients with mucus retention who can't expectorate effectively. If expiratory force is low or tracheobronchial collapse is present, combine with other techniques. ACBT improves sputum volume and reduces dyspnoea.

Jones & Rowe 2000 · Zisi 2022
B
LEVEL 2++
Recommend

Positive expiratory pressure (PEP) breathing

↑ functional residual capacity, ↓ airway resistance, opens closed small airways. Recommended for patients with excessive mucus production.

Bellone 2002 · Tang 2010
B
LEVEL 2++
May be considered

Positive-pressure oscillative breathing

Flutter/Acapella devices — as effective as postural drainage / FET in small trials, with more durable clearance. Long-term efficacy in COPD still under-studied.

Bellone 2000 · Alghamdi 2023
C
LEVEL 2−
Adjunct only

Exercise (for mucus transport)

Stimulates mucus transport via cilia beat frequency and expiratory airflow — but is not as effective as coughing and does not replace dedicated clearance techniques.

Oldenburg 1979 · Donaldson 2019
04 · Chapter 4

Physical activity & exercise training

Exacerbations accelerate the loss of quadriceps strength and fat-free mass that's already under way in stable COPD — and a drop in activity after an exacerbation independently predicts re-hospitalisation.

C

The core rule: timing matters more than the modality

Physical activity and exercise training are not recommended during uncompensated respiratory failure. Once the patient enters the sub-acute phase, walking or weight-bearing exercise should be initiated to counter deconditioning. Level 2+ · Pitta 2006, Shibuya 2022

D
LEVEL 2−
Recommend

Body positioning during exercise

Forward leaning alleviates dyspnoea during exertion, same mechanism as at rest.

Elbehairy 2022
C
LEVEL 1−
Selected patients

Breathing exercise during training

Pursed-lip breathing, slow/deep breathing and active expiration can improve exercise tolerance in patients unable to undertake conventional training.

Holland 2012
C
LEVEL 2+
Recommend

Walking aids (rollator)

Doesn't change SpO₂ but reduces the sensation of dyspnoea and improves self-paced walk distance — likely via the forward-lean position it encourages.

Solway 2002 · Vaes 2015
B
LEVEL 2++
Recommend

Lower-extremity strengthening

Safe and feasible even during an exacerbation; counteracts skeletal-muscle dysfunction without raising systemic inflammation markers. Start as the exacerbation resolves to protect daily activity and cut exertional dyspnoea.

Troosters 2010 · Saey 2011
C
LEVEL 2+
Depends on subgroup

Supplemental oxygen during exercise

On resting LTOT: likely needs O₂ above resting levels during exercise. No desaturation: routine O₂ not recommended. Exercise-induced desaturation only: O₂ can improve post-exercise dyspnoea and QoL.

Bradley 2007 · Rooyackers 1997
C
LEVEL 2+
Selected patients

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES)

For patients with severely impaired muscle strength who can't participate in regular exercise — especially those who cannot get out of bed. Preserves quadriceps strength and mass, improves dyspnoea and walking distance.

Zanotti 2003 · Trooster 2010
05 · Chapter 5

Evaluation, documentation & patient education

Closing the loop: track progress, write it down consistently, and hand the patient the skills to manage their next exacerbation earlier.

Re-evaluate against
  • Dyspnoea scale
  • Limb muscle strength
  • Self-management & mucus-clearance efficacy
Document every episode
  • History taking & physical examination
  • Dyspnoea scale at rest / on exercise, O₂ supplement + SpO₂
  • Respiratory pattern — rate, paradoxical thoraco-abdominal movement, accessory muscle use
  • Auscultation findings, coughing effort, muscle strength, BODE index
  • Problem list → treatment plan → post-treatment response
Topics for a patient education programme
  • COPD fact sheet: pathophysiology, treatment approach, self-management
  • Medication adherence & inhaler technique
  • Smoking cessation
  • Increasing physical activity & exercise
  • Managing breathlessness & energy conservation
  • Avoiding aggravating factors (smoke, pollution) & stress management
  • When to seek help; self-management, advance directives & end-of-life planning
C
LEVEL 2++
Recommend

Self-management education

Associated with fewer hospital admissions and better health-related quality of life, without excess mortality risk. Needs trained staff to select patients and provide structured follow-up — simply handing out leaflets isn't enough.

Didactic-only education (information transfer alone, no skills training) shows minimal effect on quality of life, healthcare utilisation, exercise capacity or lung function — pair facts with practised self-management skills.

Effing 2009 · Schrijver 2022 · Blackstock & Webster

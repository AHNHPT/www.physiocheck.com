Acupuncture in Physiotherapy

Physiotherapists in the Hospital Authority do not practise Traditional Chinese Acupuncture. They practise Modern Acupuncture: a needle used as an adjunct, chosen on a biomedical clinical diagnosis, with traditional concepts subsumed only where they can be. This guide walks a junior therapist through that distinction, the evidence behind it, and the governance around it.

Section 1 · Introduction, history & definition

Two thousand years old, and still being tested

Acupuncture entered physiotherapy recently, as a modality for pain syndromes and neurological conditions. Its origins are much older — and knowing where the practice came from is what lets you explain, precisely, how yours differs from it.

How long it has been practised
  • China2000+ yrs
  • Vietnam~2000 yrs
  • Korea1500+ yrs
  • Japan1450+ yrs

First described in China in the Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine (黃帝內經), dated to about 200 BC. The practice is rooted in Daoist philosophy — change, growth, balance and harmony — and rests on natural law, the balance of Yin and Yang (陰陽) and the Five Elements/Phases (五行).

What the word actually means

A European seventeenth-century label for 針灸 — strictly, the use of needles to treat or prevent symptoms and conditions.

Section 2 · Theoretical bases

The same needle, two different arguments

Traditional Chinese Acupuncture and Modern (Western Medical) Acupuncture are not two styles of needling. They differ at the level of the diagnosis that justifies the needle. Compare them row by row — this is the distinction you will be asked to defend.

Traditional Chinese Acupuncture
  • Part of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)
  • Holistic approach emphasising the unity of the human body and its environment
  • Illness follows hindrance or obstruction of Qi (氣) flowing along the meridians (經絡)
Theoretical basis
Modern / Western Medical Acupuncture
  • The scientific application of acupuncture as a therapy following orthodox clinical diagnosis
  • Built on modern neuroanatomy and physiology
  • Used by doctors and physiotherapists as an adjunctive intervention
Traditional
  • Four inspections: visual inspection (望)
  • Listening and smelling (聞)
  • Questioning (問)
  • Palpation (切)
Patient assessment
Modern
  • Systematic evaluation of clinical history and physical examination
  • Supplementary information including radiographic and laboratory findings
  • All of it directed at reaching a clinical diagnosis
Traditional
  • Points chosen on meridian theory
Selection of acupoints
Modern
  • Chosen on positive results of empirical studies for the particular condition
  • A combination of trigger points, tender points, segmental points — and traditional points
Traditional
  • Restoring free flow of Qi and the balance of Yin and Yang inside the body
Intended therapeutic effect
Modern
  • Sensory stimulation triggering complex neurophysiological and neuropharmacological responses
  • Endorphin release as the hypothesised major mechanism of the analgesic effect

Section 2.2 · The biomedical rationale

Why a needle produces analgesia

This is the reasoning that replaces meridian theory in your notes: stimulation of A-delta afferents produces analgesia at the segmental level, while higher stimulation of the mid-brain releases enkephalin and endorphin, activating a descending pain inhibition system.

Two levels, one pathway. Segmental analgesia is local and fast; the mid-brain route is systemic and slower. Both are describable in a physiotherapy note without invoking a single traditional concept — which is exactly the point.

Why the reviews disagree

Seven recurring problems in acupuncture systematic reviews

  • Incomplete literature search
  • Poorly described or inadequate inclusion criteria
  • No definition of what counted as “acupuncture”
  • Inconsistent assessment of study quality
  • No assessment of whether the acupuncture given was adequate
  • Impact of low sample size
  • Inappropriate evaluation of results

Many of the underlying trials are also methodologically flawed. What the field needs is not more reviews but new, high-quality RCTs meeting STRICTA reporting standards.

The interpretation trap

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

A review that finds no evidence of effect has not shown the treatment to be ineffective. Be careful with that step in a case conference — it is the single most common misreading of the table above, and it cuts both ways: an inconclusive verdict neither licenses nor forbids the needle. It puts the decision back on your clinical indication.

Section 3.2 · Safety

Relatively safe — because of the precautions, not instead of them

Reported incidence of serious side-effects
1:10,000—1:100,000

Surveys place serious side-effects in this range. Acupuncture is reported to be a relatively safe form of treatment with a low incidence of serious adverse events.

The clinically useful part

Serious adverse effects reported in the literature may easily be prevented by straightforward precautions.

Which is why safety precautions sit inside the Operation Guidelines, and why every event goes through the incident reporting system rather than staying in the cubicle.

Section 4 · Acupuncture practice in physiotherapy

Where you stand on the traditional–biomedical spectrum

In Hong Kong, physiotherapists practise acupuncture with distinguishable differences from practice based on TCM, under subsection 3 of Section 108(2) of the Chinese Medicine Ordinance CAP 549. Birch and Felt (1999) described five positions between the two worlds. Select a level to read it.

Level 4 · Physiotherapy position

Adherence to and belief in biomedical concepts, with the subsuming of traditional concepts where they can be subsumed. Physiotherapists adhere to biomedical concepts while allowing acupuncture concepts based on TCM to be subsumed — which is what gives the practice its de facto distinguishable difference from Traditional Chinese Acupuncture.

What Level 4 means at the plinth
  • Your diagnosis is a clinical one — history, physical examination, imaging and labs — not a pattern of disharmony.
  • Your point selection may include traditional points, but the reason you chose them is empirical evidence for that condition, alongside trigger, tender and segmental points.
  • Your documentation should read like physiotherapy: indication, clinical reasoning, response, review.
  • Level 5 is not the target either. Rejecting traditional models wholesale is not what the manual describes.

Section 5 · Quality assurance system

Five things that stand between competence and a needle

Established by the Coordinating Committee in Physiotherapy of the Hospital Authority to uphold the standards and quality of acupuncture practice by physiotherapists.

5.1 · Accreditation

Accreditation system

All physiotherapists who work and practise acupuncture in the Hospital Authority must go through the accreditation procedures set out by the Hong Kong Physiotherapy Association (HKPA), whose standards are benchmarked with the International Acupuncture Association of Physical Therapists (IAAPT).

5.2 · Guidelines

Operation guidelines

The Operation Guidelines for Physiotherapists Practising Acupuncture in Hospital Authority assures standards of practice and safety precautions. Physiotherapists also adhere to the HKPA Practice Guidelines on Acupuncture by Physiotherapists in Hong Kong 2003.

5.3 · Protocols

Condition protocols

Intervention protocols for specific conditions such as osteoarthritis and stroke are under development by the Working Group on Acupuncture, serving as practice guides for those conditions.

5.4 · Incidents

Incident reporting

An incident reporting system records and evaluates incidents and adverse events in acupuncture practice. This is the mechanism that turns the safety literature into local learning.

5.5 · Education

Continuing education

Physiotherapists working in the Hospital Authority are required to attend continuing education programmes on contemporary developments in Modern Acupuncture.

Section 6 · The way forward

Training, precautions, evidence

With necessary training and accreditation, strict adherence to safety precautions, and the application of evolving evidence from high-quality clinical trials, more HA physiotherapists will be equipped to practise safe and effective Modern Acupuncture.

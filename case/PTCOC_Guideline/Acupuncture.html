01 — Origins

Two thousand years, two frameworks

Acupuncture's history runs long before physiotherapy adopted it — and that history is exactly why two distinct theoretical schools exist side by side today.

≈200 BC

First described in China's Huang Di Nei Jing (Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine).

1450–2000 yrs

Independently practised across Japan, Korea and Vietnam for well over a millennium each.

Late 1600s

The word "acupuncture" is coined in Europe, from Latin acus (needle) + punctura (to puncture).

Today

Practised in Hospital Authority physiotherapy as an adjunctive modality for pain and neurological conditions.

針灸
Working definition

Acupuncture is rooted in the Daoist principles of Yin–Yang balance and the Five Elements — but as physiotherapists use it, the term simply refers to the use of needles to treat or prevent symptoms and conditions, applied on the basis of an orthodox clinical diagnosis rather than a meridian-based one.

02 — Two roots, one practice

Traditional Chinese Acupuncture vs. Modern Acupuncture

Physiotherapists in Hong Kong practise Modern Acupuncture (Western Medical Acupuncture) — a distinct discipline from Traditional Chinese Acupuncture, though both use the same needles.

Traditional Chinese Acupuncture

Qi, meridians, balance

Theoretical basis

Part of TCM; a holistic approach centred on the unity of the body and its environment.

Patient assessment

The four inspections — looking (望), listening/smelling (聞), questioning (問), palpation (切).

Point selection

Based on meridian theory.

Intended effect

Restoring free flow of Qi and the balance of Yin and Yang.

Modern Acupuncture

Diagnosis, neurophysiology, evidence

Theoretical basis

Scientific application of acupuncture following orthodox clinical diagnosis; grounded in neuroanatomy and physiology.

Patient assessment

Clinical history, physical examination, and supplementary findings (imaging, labs) reaching a clinical diagnosis.

Point selection

Trigger points, tender points, segmental points and traditional points, chosen from empirical study results.

Intended effect

Sensory stimulation triggering neurophysiological and neuropharmacological responses; endorphin release is the leading hypothesised mechanism.

How it works, in modern terms: needling stimulates A-delta afferents to produce analgesia at the segmental (spinal) level, and stimulates the mid-brain to release enkephalin and endorphin at a higher level — activating a descending pain-inhibition system.

03 — Reading the evidence

Let the evidence set the ceiling, not the floor

This guideline's clinical rule of thumb: don't offer acupuncture where the evidence is conclusively negative; use it adjunctively where evidence is inconclusive and clinically indicated; and where evidence is conclusively positive, it may even become the primary intervention — always weighed against the individual's indications and contraindications.

Why is the acupuncture evidence base so messy?
  • Incomplete literature searches
  • Poorly described or inadequate inclusion criteria
  • No consistent definition of "acupuncture" across studies
  • Inconsistent quality assessment of included studies
  • No assessment of whether the acupuncture "dose" given was adequate
  • Low sample sizes
  • Inappropriate evaluation of results

04 — Where physiotherapy practice sits

Distinct from TCM, by law and by framework

Physiotherapists don't practise acupuncture as a lesser version of TCM — it's a separately defined scope of practice, both legally in Hong Kong and conceptually.

Legal basis

Under Chinese Medicine Ordinance CAP 549, s.108(2)(3), physiotherapists in Hong Kong practise acupuncture with distinguishable differences from TCM-based practice. Within this framework, physiotherapists practise Modern Acupuncture, not Traditional Chinese Medicine.

Birch & Felt (1999) frame acupuncture practice along a five-level spectrum, from full adherence to traditional concepts through to full adherence to biomedical concepts. Tap a level to read it — physiotherapy practice consistently sits at Level 4.

Level 4 — Adherence to and belief in biomedical concepts, with traditional TCM concepts subsumed only where they fit that biomedical model. This is the level at which physiotherapists practise, giving their acupuncture a de facto difference from Traditional Chinese Acupuncture.

05 — Five pillars of safe practice

How HA keeps acupuncture accountable

A quality assurance system, established by the Coordinating Committee in Physiotherapy, underpins every physiotherapist's acupuncture practice in HA.

Accreditation

All HA physiotherapists practising acupuncture must complete accreditation set out by the Hong Kong Physiotherapy Association (HKPA), benchmarked against the International Acupuncture Association of Physical Therapists (IAAPT).

Operation guidelines

HA's Operation Guidelines for Physiotherapists Practising Acupuncture plus HKPA's 2003 Practice Guidelines on Acupuncture set the standards of practice and safety precautions.

Condition protocols

Intervention protocols for specific conditions — such as osteoarthritis and stroke — are being developed by the Working Group on Acupuncture to guide condition-specific practice.

Incident reporting

A dedicated incident reporting system records and evaluates every incident and adverse event arising from acupuncture practice.

Continuing education

Physiotherapists are required to keep attending continuing education programmes on contemporary developments in Modern Acupuncture.

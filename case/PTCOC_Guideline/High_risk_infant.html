i Overview

Why this guideline exists

Since January 1993, the territory-wide Very Low Birth Weight (VLBW, <1.5 kg) Babies Outcome Surveillance Programme has tracked developmental outcomes of VLBW infants across all HA hospitals and Child Assessment Centres, assessed historically at 18 months corrected age. As individual hospitals developed their own high-risk infant programmes, the Physiotherapy Coordinating Committee Paediatric Specialty Group built this manual to streamline practice across clusters and care settings.

Reading the evidence tags used throughout this guide

1 Identify

Identifying the high-risk infant

Three overlapping groups trigger high-risk follow-up: prematurity, infants with special-needs red flags, and infants who have undergone major surgery.

1.1 Birthweight → mortality (Stevenson et al, 1998)

CP prevalence drops sharply beyond 32 weeks — but Boyle & Boyle (2013) argue moderate (32–33wk) and late (34–36wk) preterm infants still warrant follow-up, since subtler long-term outcomes persist.

1.2 Red-flag checklist beyond prematurity (Purdy, 2012)

! Apgar <4 at 5 minutes
! IVH > Grade II
! Congenital anomalies / birth defects
! Hyperbilirubinaemia near exchange levels
! Sepsis, meningitis
! Intracranial abnormality (e.g. HIE)
! Abnormal neuro exam (tone, tremor)
! Seizures
! Hypoglycaemia requiring intervention
! Persistent pulmonary HTN needing NO
! Home on apnoea monitor + caffeine
! ECMO · or infant of substance-using mother
Recommendation · Inclusion criteria
  • Prematurity: ≤32 weeks gestation or birthweight ≤1500 g
  • Infants with special needs (red-flag checklist above)
  • Infants who have undergone major surgery — cardiac or non-cardiac (Walker et al, 2012)
2 Timing

Timing of intervention

A 2018 internal survey of six HA hospitals running high-risk infant services found intervention starts as early as 30 weeks PMA when stable, with most units beginning around 32–34 weeks.

“Since the late 1970s the NICU environment has been evaluated with regard to the types and amounts of sensory stimulation. The general consensus is that most infants receive neither sensory deprivation nor sensory overstimulation, but an inappropriate pattern of interaction that includes the components of both.” Barb & Lemons, 1989
Recommendation

Consider starting intervention once postmenstrual age exceeds 30 weeks and the infant is medically stable.

Recommendation

Cluster handling with general care wherever possible, to protect a diurnal sleep–wake cycle rather than adding isolated handling episodes.

3 NICU Care

Strategies to support growth & development in the perinatal period

Six evidence domains — from reading the infant's own stability signals to feeding readiness. Use the tabs to move between them.

The Synactive Theory of Infant Development (Als, 1982) frames the preterm infant's behaviour as five interdependent subsystems. Reading stability versus stress signals in each is the foundation of every handling decision that follows.

Signs of stress
Autonomic

Recommendation

Train yourself and caregivers to notice fragility/stress signals versus robustness/stability signals across all five subsystems, then adapt environment and handling accordingly (Blanchard et al, 2015).

Level I

Mechanical ventilation

Rivas-Fernandez et al (2016): low–moderate quality evidence favours prone for slightly improved oxygenation, but no position shows sustained, clinically relevant benefit.

Level I Level III‑1

Apnoea / spontaneous breathing

Ballout et al (2017): insufficient evidence overall. Gouna et al (2013, n=19): prone / left-lateral with nest support raised SpO₂ and tidal volume vs supine via better thoracoabdominal synchrony.

Level I

IVH prevention

Romantsik et al (2017): only two RCTs identified (midline vs 90° head rotation); no clear effect shown, and no trials compared supine vs prone midline. More research needed.

SIDS risk

King & Norton (2017): prone aids neurodevelopment, sleep state and physiological stability in hospital — but parents must be taught safe, supine home-sleep practice before discharge.

Level III‑1

Gastro-oesophageal reflux

Ewer (1999) & van Wijk (2007): prone and left-lateral positions consistently reduced reflux episode number and duration versus supine and right-lateral.

Level II

Plagiocephaly

Aarnivala et al (2015, n=111 RCT): structured positioning/handling education halved deformational plagiocephaly at 3 months (2D 11% vs 31%; 3D 15% vs 33%).

Recommendation

Positioning is a genuinely complex trade-off between cardiorespiratory stability, neuromusculoskeletal development and pathophysiology. Parent education on appropriate versus inappropriate sleep positioning before discharge is essential to prevent SIDS while preserving developmental gains made in hospital.

Level I

Li Wang et al, 2013

17 studies: massage improved daily weight gain by 5.32 g and shortened length of stay by 4.41 days.

Level I

Badr et al, 2015

34 studies (PRISMA): weight gain +0.53 g/day and mental scores +7.89 points; no significant effect on LOS or caloric intake.

Level I

Alvarez et al, 2017

23 studies: benefits linked to ↑vagal tone, ↑gastric activity, ↑insulin — plus better neurodevelopment, fewer sepsis episodes, shorter stay, reduced stress.

Level II

Haley et al, 2012

RCT: tactile + kinesthetic stimulation increased bone turnover in favour of bone accretion.

Recommendation

Massage, tactile and kinesthetic stimulation are low-cost, near-zero-risk modalities with real potential to support weight gain and development.

Level II

White-Traut et al, 2014 (HOPE)

Twice-daily ATVV + maternal guidance → more orally-directed behaviours (rooting, sucking) and more time alert after 7 days.

Level II

Medoff-Cooper et al, 2015

n=183: sucks, sucks-per-burst and sucking maturity index all improved significantly faster than control.

Level II

White-Traut et al, 2002

n=37: increased alertness within 5 minutes, faster nippled intake, shorter length of stay.

Recommendation

Structured Auditory-Tactile-Visual-Vestibular stimulation has real potential to improve state organisation and oral feeding readiness.

Level II

Ustad et al, 2016

Multi-centre RCT: parents taught postural-control activities at 34 weeks PMA, delivered under PT supervision to 37 weeks → greater TIMP score gain than control.

Level I

Schulzke et al, 2014

4-trial meta-analysis: physical activity programmes (with adequate nutrition) show suggestive short-term weight gain and bone mineralisation benefit.

Recommendation

Exercise / physical activity programmes have potential benefit for both motor development and bone mineralisation in premature infants.

Neonatal feeding difficulty spans neurological, pulmonary and aerodigestive causes, presenting as overt or silent aspiration (Jadcherla, 2017); videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS) clarifies the mechanism.

Prematurity predisposes to breathing and oral-development problems: mouth-breathing disorganises sucking (Trabalon & Schaal, 2012), and low-tone rest posture can drive a high, narrow palate (Moeller, 2010). Myofunctional therapy targets lip seal, palatal tongue rest, pharyngeal tone and functional posture.

Assess: tongue tie Assess: mouth breathing Assess: high-arch palate Assess: orofacial dysfunction
Recommendation

Central alignment of the body underpins effective oral sensorimotor and feeding activity (Arvedson, 2002). Physiotherapists play an essential role in managing aerodigestive dysfunction.

4 Parents

Parental education for transition to home

Care doesn't end at the incubator — the strongest short-term gains come from equipping parents before discharge.

Level II

Ustad et al, 2016

Parent-administered PT under supervision improved TIMP scores versus control.

Level II

White-Traut et al, 2014

Nurse–community advocate guidance alongside ATVV boosted orally-directed behaviour and alertness.

Level I

Vanderveen et al, 2016

25-study meta-analysis: parent-involved early intervention improved neurodevelopment at 12 months, sustained to 3 years — but no longer detectable at age 5, and gains were stronger for mental than physical scores.

Recommendation

Parent education before transition home has real potential to improve short-term developmental outcomes. Parental involvement in delivering care is crucial and highly recommended — but set expectations that the measurable advantage may fade by school age.

5 Screening

Follow-up screening of neuro-sensorimotor development

HA's internal consensus: screen every 4 months in year one corrected age, then every 6 months in year two. When Novak's (2017) more frequent 3–4-month screening protocol was implemented clinically (Byrne et al, 2017), average CP diagnosis age fell from 18 months to 13 months.

6 Referral

Referral pathways

Physiotherapy-identified motor abnormality is only the start of the pathway — HA services connect outward to the Department of Health and Social Welfare Department.

Stage 1

Early intervention

Physiotherapy (± other disciplines) begins within HA once motor abnormality is identified at screening.

→
Stage 2a

EETC

NGO-run Early Education & Training Centre for birth–2y (incl. Training Subsidy Programme for those waitlisted). Family-centred programme; PT → medical officer → social worker → Central Referral System.

→
Stage 2b

CAC assessment

Child Assessment Centre — part of the territory-wide VLBW Outcome Surveillance Programme since 1993. ELBW (<1.0kg) infants assessed at 8 & 21 months corrected age per protocol.

→
Stage 3

SCCC placement

Considered when moderate–severe abnormality is likely to persist beyond age 2. Referral flows through CAC assessment, then social worker → Central Referral System for Rehabilitation Services.

7 Exit

Exit of the follow-up — and beyond

Milner et al (2015) reviewed VLBW survivor outcomes; follow-up duration in the literature ranged from 6 months to 13 years.

21.4%

Overall neurodevelopmental impairment (95% CI 11.6–30.8)

16.3%

Cognitive impairment (95% CI 6.3–29.6)

11.2%

Cerebral palsy (95% CI 5.9–16.1)

18 → 13 months

Earlier, more frequent (3–4 month) screening moved the average age of CP diagnosis from 18 months down to 13 months in the follow-up clinic (Byrne et al, 2017).

DCD at school age

Williams et al (2009): 40% mild-moderate and 19% moderate motor-skill impairment (excluding CP) in preterm (<37wk) children — versus a 5–8% DCD prevalence in the general school-age population (Barnhart, 2003). These infants are easy to lose from view once discharged from early follow-up.

Follow-up duration is “a compromise … between assessing children at early ages … and assessing children later in life, allowing more detailed assessment but running the risk of loss to follow-up.” Doyle et al, 2014
Practice point

Most neurological impairment or motor delay is identifiable by age 2, though some recommend extending follow-up to 3 years for ELBW (<1.0 kg) infants (Lee et al, 2008). Infants with abnormalities identified before age 2 continue receiving HA multidisciplinary care until EETC or SCCC placement is available — this requires close, ongoing collaboration between Hospital Authority, the Department of Health and the Social Welfare Department.

📋Discussion:

The diagnosis of this case was cellulitis.

This case was referred to FM Clinic and given a course of Ampicillin and Cloxacillin to treat the underlying infection. The patient's wife also developed foot cellulitis two days after the patient was diagnosed. The messy home environment may lead to poor hygiene. Home cleansing (especially home slippers) was recommended.

Acute stroke treatments and the physiological stress of a stroke itself frequently trigger gout flare-ups. Up to 4% of hospitalized stroke patients experience a gout attack within the first week. These flares commonly present in the paretic (weakened) limb and can complicate recovery. [1]

Why Strokes Trigger Gout Flares?

1. Post-Stroke Inflammation: Acute stroke causes massive systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which accelerates purine metabolism and causes sudden spikes in serum uric acid levels.

2. Medications Used in Stroke Care:

2A) Diuretics: Blood pressure-lowering "water pills" (e.g., hydrochlorothiazide) are common after strokes but impair the kidneys' ability to excrete uric acid.

2B) Low-Dose Aspirin: Often prescribed after ischemic strokes, it can cause unpredictable shifts in uric acid levels.

3. Dehydration & Immobility: Routine fluid restriction during stroke recovery or immobility can concentrate uric acid in the blood and joints.

In this case, the initial suspicion of gout is reasonable given the onset of pain and medical comorbidities. However, a lack of treatment progress and the progression of the painful area should lead to a re-evaluation of the diagnosis, potentially shifting it from gout to cellulitis.

⭐️Learning point:

  • -Some drugs may increase the likelihood of gout
  • -Diagnosis is fluid to change with clinical picture progress
  • -When treatment trial did not provide the expect effect, clinical team members should consider alternate possibilities
  • -DDx between cellulitis and gouty attack

👍Explanation:

Q1.Option 1: Check vitals

Raised body temperature is a crucial hint of severe or systemic infection. In this case, if the patient is febrile/septic looking on visit, the patient should be advised to AED instead of FMC for further management.

Please also bear in mind that a febrile condition may be masked by medications (e.g. NSAID/Panadol).

The patient also reported an episode of chills. Chills and rigours are your body's involuntary response to a sudden shift in temperature, usually signalling an underlying infection.

Q1.Option 2: Inspect R foot

Both cellulitis and gouty attacks have skin discolouration—redness on light skin tones; on brown or black skin, it may look purple, grey, or darker than the surrounding skin.

However, gout should stay localised (most commonly the big toe, ankle, or knee). Cellulitis often spreads diffusely. Dimpling around hair follicles may give an "orange peel" texture.[2]

Q1.Option 3: Palpation to R foot

The area must be palpated for fluctuance (a wave-like feeling). A localised abscess requires incision and drainage, whereas pure cellulitis is treated with antibiotics. If fluctuance is detected in this patient, he should be advised to AED instead of FMC.

Q1.Option 4: Check capillary refill of toes

Checking the capillary refill time (CRT) of an affected limb or extremity is a standard component of evaluating a patient with cellulitis.[2] It helps clinicians assess tissue perfusion, monitor for severe complications, and differentiate cellulitis from other vascular conditions.

Q2.Option 1&2: Cont'd meds and review 2 days later/ Book FMC

Patient's clinical picture flavours the suspect of cellulitis instead of gout. Continue current medication cannot take the underlying infection and delaying the indicated treatment.

Q2.Option 3: Ice and compression to Rt foot

No, although studies shows that compression therapy is effective to alleviate symptoms without causing complications in cellulitis, compression should be started with a time window to allow the antibiotic therapy to take effect before initiating compression therapy. Moreover, if wound or ulcer is present in the cellulitis site, ice or compression maybe contraindicated. [3] In patients with initial CRP levels above 50 mg/dl at admission, CRP reduction occurred more rapidly.

Q2.Option 4: Withhold walking

Walking is allowed in view of bone involvement being less likely in this case. The patient reported no trauma/injury history, making an underlying fracture less likely.

In severe cases of cellulitis, the infection can spread into deeper tissues, potentially leading to osteomyelitis (OM). In this case, an X-ray is needed to rule out any osteomyelitic changes.

Case: Bedbound patient after stroke

Mr. Chan, a 90 years old man, suffered from bilateral MCA infarct.
He had been hospitalized for 6 months due to stroke, pneumonia and septic shock.
A course of rehabilitation was given to patient in rehabilitation hospital.
Refer outreach physiotherapy service for home exercise.

Case details:
Social Hx:
Live in lift-landing flat with maid.
Patient's son and daughter visit patient daily.
Premorbid walk with stick

PMH: HT on Losartan and Betaloc, CKD, AF on apixaban

HPI:
Admitted for CVA
Present with sudden onset aphasia and dull looking
Clinically gaze deviation to Left side
Given IVT in AED
CTA showed no LVO
Developed new Left hemiparesis the next day after admission
Repeated CT brain showed bilateral MCA territory infarct
Consulted neurosurgery: no intervention
Started on tube feeding
Later developed pneumonia and septic shock. Completed a course of Tazocin.
Once required BiPAP and dopamine for hemodynamic support but later weaned off.

A course of rehabilitation was given to patient in rehabilitation hospital
Upon discharge, he was bedbound with R/T feeding.

Family's caring plan for patient:
-Patient’s family wishes to continue home care.
-Patient is cared by maid in ADL (grooming, wiping, changing diaper).
-They hired private outreach Chinese medical practitioner for acupuncture and rehabilitation 5 times per week.

In the first home visit, patient’s maid, son and daughter are present.

O/E:
BP 110/75 Pulse 91 SpO2 96% on room air. Afebrile.

Learning point:


Prioritize safety & prevention over ambitious goals in very severe, chronic cases (biopsychosocial approach, realistic expectations).

Question 1:
A. Screening of skin condition
This patient is a bedbound, advanced age patient with a prolonged period of hospitalization. It increases the likelihood of developing pressure sore. The common site of the bedsore is as below.


Preventive measure could be considered
1. Regular repositioning
2. Use of pressure relieving support surface( mattress, wheelchair cushion, etc.)
3. Protection on bony prominence (such as the use of heel protectors, pillow)
4. Skin care and moisture
5. Nutrition(adequate protein, calories, vitamin) and hydration
6. Regular risk assessment (such as Braden Scale)

B. ROM, muscle strength, muscle tone
They are core physiotherapy assessments in severe stroke.
Identifies contractures, spasticity, pain, or severe weakness (very common after prolonged immobility and bilateral MCA damage).
Establishes baseline for realistic goal setting (e.g., rolling, bridging, supine → sit, assisted transfers).
Guides prevention of complications (pressure ulcers, joint deformity, pain, chest infection).

C. Functional capacity (bed mobility, transfer and mobility)
Functional capacity assessment is emphasized in almost all stroke rehab guidelines for severe cases. It provide the basis
for the therapist to formulate a treatment plan and rehabilitation goal with the patient and his family.

D. Home environment, availability of assistive tools and walking aids
This is a key difference from hospital-based assessment.
In a lift-landing flat with maid as primary carer, environmental factors determine safety, feasibility of any intervention, and carer burden (e.g., bed height, space for hoisting, turning, pressure-relieving mattress, availability of slide sheets, transfer belts, commode, etc.).
Even though the patient is bedbound, assessment of potential for future progression (e.g., hoist availability, future wheelchair consideration) is important.
Guidelines (NICE, VA/DoD, Canadian Stroke Best Practice) strongly recommend environmental assessment as part of
community/home rehab.

Question 2:
A. Carer training in turning, transfer and limbs maintenance
This is a core priority in home-based care for severe stroke with high carer dependence.
NICE Guideline NG236 explicitly recommends offering training to family/carers in moving/handling, transfers, positioning,
and care (including limb maintenance to prevent contractures/pain).
Canadian Stroke Best Practices emphasize carer education as essential for safety, reducing burden, preventing complications (e.g., pressure ulcers from poor positioning, contractures from unmanaged tone).
With maid as primary carer and daily family visits, training in safe turning (to prevent shear/friction), assisted transfers (using transfer belt/board), and passive ROM/maintenance for the spastic right side and flaccid left side is critical and realistic in the first session.
Teaching should include cues for the aphasic patient (gestures, yes/no, visual aids) and monitoring for fatigue/pain.

B. Bedside sitting and trunk control exercise
Appropriate and evidence-supported as an initial, low-risk intervention.
The patient has fair neck control but poor static sitting balance (leans left), requires 1 moderate-heavy assist for lying-to-sitting.
Guidelines (NICE, Canadian) support early/repetitive task-specific practice for sitting balance in severe cases, starting with supported bedside sitting to build trunk control, prevent deconditioning, and improve tolerance for ADLs/pressure relief.
This is goal-directed and safe with assistance available (son/daughter/maid). Use the electric bed for gradual elevation,
monitor vitals (given age, AF, CKD), and limit duration to avoid fatigue/hypotension.
Focus in this case should be on bed-based interventions initially (positioning, passive movements, respiratory care, basic bed mobility).

C. Application of barrier cream on the skin of potential bedsore
A barrier cream creates a protective layer on the skin to shield it from irritants like moisture (urine/feces), chemicals, or friction, preventing dryness, damage, and conditions like incontinence-associated dermatitis or occupational dermatitis,
while also locking in moisture and promoting healing for compromised skin. It works by forming a physical shield, allowing the skin to breathe, and is thicker than a regular moisturizer, often containing ingredients like zinc oxide or ceramides to support the skin's natural barrier.
It is not routine care for bedbound patient. It would be helpful if the patient presents with indications such as incontinence-associated dermatitis or stage 1 pressure injury.

D. Fall advice, care advice and prevention of complication (DVT)
Prevention of secondary complications is a top priority in severe, bedbound elderly stroke patients.
Fall prevention: Even though the patient is bedbound, advice should be given on safe transfer, and environmental hazards.
Supervision must be given in sitting exercise in this case.
DVT: Risk factors include immobility, age, AF. Advise ankle pumps, compression stocking if appropriate. Education should
be done to the carer on when to seek medical review for swelling/pain.
NICE and Canadian guidelines all stress education on complication prevention, carer advice, and risk management as foundational in community/home settings.
